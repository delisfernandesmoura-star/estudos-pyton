Part II

Foundations of Singing

Terminology, voice classification, styles, breathing, and the physiology that sustains the singing voice.

Chapter 6The Vocabulary of Singing

This chapter introduces the terms used in professional singing. Understanding them is the first step toward real command of your performance.

Music
The harmonic, melodic and rhythmic combination of musical sounds.
Melody
A combination of successive sounds, heard one after another, as in a scale.
Register
Register is directly linked to the resonance of the sound. Low and middle notes sound most natural with chest resonance; in the upper range, resonance is felt predominantly in the face and head.

The singer’s goal is a homogeneous voice, one that unites the low, middle and high registers into a single color and sound. In vocal training it is essential to blend two or more registers so that the transitions between them become imperceptible. This is achieved through scales and proper breath support, together with a deep understanding of how to stabilize the column of air. Vowels gradually modify as the voice moves through its range, and they play a fundamental role in keeping the larynx in a stable position, balancing low, middle and high notes and bridging the registers.

Although some voice scientists prefer to speak of registers and sub-registers, this book works with three vocal registers: modal, basal (pulse) and elevated. Some theorists divide the modal register into chest and head sub-registers; here, head voice is treated as part of the elevated register. Within it, head voice corresponds to what we mistakenly call falsetto, a word that implies a “false” voice, when in fact there is no such thing as a false voice.

Modal register. The larynx sits low and the vocal folds vibrate more slowly, setting the bones of the chest into vibration; hence the term “chest voice.” This is our natural voice, the one we use for speaking.

Basal (pulse) register. It resembles the creak of a door swinging open. It can serve as an indicator of relaxed musculature (vocal fry, or the basal-sound technique).

Elevated register. Its hallmarks are a raised larynx and faster vibration of the vocal folds. It is also characterized “by energy transmitted in the form of vibratory sensations in the oral, pharyngeal and nasal cavities, that is, in the cranial vault” (DINVILLE, 1993).
The three registers
Range
Vocal range is unique to each individual: every person has a lowest and a highest note of their own. Range can be extended, both downward and upward, but doing so requires a great deal of work on the voice.
Tessitura
Essentially, the notes that occur most often in a passage of music and that lie in a more central region of the voice, one that is comfortable and natural to sing in. The more you sing within a comfortable region, the healthier it is for your voice.
Timbre
Timbre is the personality of the voice. It is bound up with harmonics: it results from the many harmonic sounds that make up each voice. The richer a voice is in harmonics, the more beautiful it sounds. Timbre is the control of “color” that lets a voice sound bright, dark, open, closed, nasal, oral, or more or less projected. It is the result of acoustic phenomena that take place in the supralaryngeal cavities.

By modifying the volume and muscle tone of these areas, as well as those of the lips and cheeks, the singer can voluntarily enrich or impoverish the fundamental sound generated by the vocal folds in the larynx. This depends on the order, number and intensity of the harmonics that accompany it, which are filtered in these cavities according to pitch and vowel.

Timbre is the quality of the voice, its identity. It is what allows us to tell one person from another: the quality and distinctiveness of the sound, the particular shape of its sound wave.
Intensity
Vocal intensity is the property that makes a sound louder or softer. Training this dynamic can give the listener an engaging sense of variety. It refers to control of the loudness of the voice, from fortissimo to pianissimo, and to every dynamic gradation in between. It depends on subglottal pressure (the abdominal support that makes vocal power possible).

Intensity is experienced as a sensation of muscle tone distributed throughout the vocal organs. It is perceived as energy transmitted, little by little, to all the resonating cavities and to the muscles of the pharynx and larynx. As they work, singers must be aware of the muscular effort that intensity demands and of the appropriate, whole-body vocal dynamic that enriches the sound spectrum. Intensity increases with vocal muscle tone and is associated with pitch.
Duration
A sound may last as long as the music calls for, whether short or sustained. Duration is the length of time a sound is produced.
Rhythm
The combination of the durations of sounds and rests within a piece of music (time signatures, tempo): a sequence of sounds organized around a pulse. Differences in style (samba, waltz, tango, rock and so on) are revealed above all by rhythm.
Tempo
The speed at which a song is performed, faster or slower.
Interpretation
Written music includes dynamic markings. In popular music, the arranger notates the dynamics the song should convey; quite often, however, it is the performer who brings his or her own variations, always guided by good taste and good judgment.
Pitch
The quality that makes a sound low, high or in between. It involves controlling the modulation of frequencies, extending the vocal range and singing in tune. To change pitch, one must change expiratory pressure, that is, modulate the degree of abdominal muscle tone, as well as the volume of the supralaryngeal cavities. These adjustments in turn alter the position of the larynx, glottal closure, the vibration frequency of the vocal folds and the place where vibration is felt.
Homogeneity
An essential quality that depends on how the zones of resonance are distributed and on how the voice’s different sonorities blend, given their constant interaction. It can be achieved only through the progressive harmonization of all the organs essential to phonation, in other words, through a system of compensation across the entire range and on every vowel, so as to soften or reinforce certain formants (the frequencies that help determine timbre).
Intonation
Intonation is the counterpart of homogeneity. It depends on well-distributed pressure and muscle tone, which ensure proper adduction of the vocal folds and the appropriate shaping of the resonating cavities. Intonation is governed by extremely delicate movements and by mastery of a set of sensations to which the singer must pay very close attention.

Some singers sing flat because they fail to sustain the breath, owing to low muscle tone; others sing sharp, either from excessive pressure or because their breath is spent too quickly.

Food for Thought

Whatever the changes in timbre, intensity or pitch, and whatever the changes in the surrounding conditions, vocal technique must remain the same. The singer need only adapt it to these different parameters.

From a functional standpoint, the carrying power of the voice is always related to the energy expended. It shows itself chiefly in an awareness of muscle tone throughout the whole body, in sharper proprioceptive sensations and in a richer acoustic play of timbre.

Unlike other instruments, the voice has no physical form one can see or touch, and for that reason it is harder to control. This is why singers must have at their disposal a secure and conscious technique, grounded in precise sensations and movements, so that they never lose command of their voice, wherever they happen to be singing.

We can say, then, that the essential condition for a voice that carries is control of internal sensations. It is above all the muscular sensations that tell us what the organs are doing. The work of the muscles, the movements of the organs and the awareness of vibratory tremor are all linked to the propagation of the sound wave through the pharyngeal cavity, along the musculomembranous wall that runs in front of the spine. This is the “place of the voice,” as singers call it: a place they seek out with the help of humming, to arrive at a sound and a vibratory sensation that is felt differently depending on how the larynx moves, the harmonic richness of the laryngeal sound and the pitch. Listeners do not perceive these sensations, but they are essential to the singer. After long training, they become an effective means of control.

These sensations must be complemented by auditory-phonatory control, that is, by listening to one’s own voice, since acoustic output is monitored by the inner ear, which makes it possible to evaluate and appreciate the quality of the sounds produced. The singer thus draws, on the one hand, on the ability to control the voice heard through the air and, on the other, on a set of oral, pharyngeal, nasal and laryngeal vibrations that reach the inner ear through bone conduction. For low notes, these vibrations travel by way of the muscles that lower the larynx; for high notes, by way of the muscles that raise it.

Auditory sensations vary with the venue and its acoustics. Sometimes the singer feels the voice is ringing too much; at other times he or she can barely hear it, and the voice seems muffled. Singers who do not want to force their voices should therefore attend to their internal sensations, both muscular and vibratory, because these do not change and do not depend on where they happen to be.

The singing voice is acquired with the help of these sensations, which arise from complex postures and relationships. Becoming conscious of them is indispensable to vocal quality and stability.

Beginners need sensory and vibratory information, delivered through exercises and complementary procedures that build the muscular coordination acting directly on breathing and, at the same time, on the vocal and articulatory organs. To make it easier to discover these different sensations, teachers use a vocabulary of imagery, metaphor and subjective evocation, together with a mental picture of the sound, its color and its mechanism, all of which indirectly bring about the right posture for phonation. The aim is to find a balance between expiratory pressure and vocal emission, expressed as a dynamic engagement of the whole body.

Chapter 7Voice Classification

Voice classification should rest primarily on anatomical, morphological and acoustic grounds. Several factors must be considered, some of them predominant and others secondary. Without a careful assessment, one that takes time and patient observation, singers run the risk of singing in a tessitura that does not suit them. Even if they find it beautiful and enjoyable to sing with a particular timbre, the consequences for their voice can be disastrous.

A voice is as individual as a fingerprint. Many singers enjoy success for a while and then develop a range of vocal symptoms, such as fatigue and hoarseness, that can become chronic and pull them away from their careers. The problem is that once an artist has become successful in a musical style that is not necessarily the best fit for his or her voice, changing that public image is extremely difficult.

Predominant factors
TessituraThe set of notes the singer can produce easily (notes sung comfortably).
Vocal rangeThe full set of notes, from lowest to highest, that the singer can produce (notes sung both with and without comfort).
Secondary factors
Respiratory capacity and the development of the chest and abdomen.
The pitch of the speaking voice, provided the person uses the pitch that matches his or her anatomical makeup.
Vocal amplitude, which indicates a voice with full, rounded sonorities across the entire range.
Intensity, which allows for power without strain.
Temperament, which represents the singer’s overall qualities in relation to his or her vocal possibilities.
Morphological characteristics. It is commonly held that tenors and sopranos tend to be short, stocky and full-figured, and that contraltos and basses tend to be slender, but there are exceptions.

Chapter 8Singing Styles

Broadly speaking, singing falls into two large families: classical singing and popular singing. Within each, we find a variety of styles.

Classical Singing

Over the course of history, classical singing has been approached in many ways, each with its own language. Yet ever since the fourth century, when chant emerged in the churches of Rome, the priorities have remained the same: mastery of the coordination between breathing, phonation and articulation; register transitions with no perceptible change in timbre; and flawless vocal agility.

Italian opera was an original art form that grew so powerful and important that the voice itself came to be cultivated intensively, and a technique was developed to perfect it. The Italian language, rich in pure vowels and with few final consonants, lent itself naturally to singing.

The castrated singers (castrati), who rose to fame in the sixteenth century, deserve mention here. Their renown spread across the European continent. The practice consisted of castrating boys at a very young age and subjecting them to relentless vocal training. The castrati were considered voices without equal. Their sound was the acoustic result of a small, boyish larynx, left undeveloped by the disruption of male sex hormones, whose fundamental frequency, generated by the vocal folds, was reinforced by the resonators of an adult body. The rest of their growth proceeded normally under the control of the pituitary gland.

Still within the classical tradition, recitative, a declaimed style of song, came to prominence in 1602; physiologically, its production resembles the singing voice. From there bel canto gradually emerged, demanding perfect technical mastery, great beauty of tone, an extended range and remarkable agility. Great volume was of little interest, since performances generally took place in palace salons, which were not as large as the theaters built later.

Reactions against bel canto began to appear, and the expressive power of the orchestra was gradually consolidated, bringing innovation to the art of instrumentation.

In the Romantic period, the voice took on great importance in expression, declamation and the portrayal of characters. Thus, in early nineteenth-century Paris, the covered, supported voice emerged, setting aside the falsetto and the gentle voice so prevalent in bel canto. These were replaced by genuine full-voiced top notes, sung by voices that brought virile, convincing characters to life.

German, French and Italian opera, and the Romantics as a whole, came to demand voices of great power, expressiveness, range and command of register. This is classical, operatic singing.

In the twentieth century, composers emerged with a style that called for singers who could not only sing but also act, dance and improvise. Along this path, belting steadily gained ground. Today we can enjoy it in the great flourishing of musical theater, including popular songs that “blend” certain elements of classical placement while bringing the resonance forward into the “mask” and producing a grittier, edgier sound (SUNDBERG; GRAMMING; LOVETRI, 1993; BJORKNER, 2008).

Popular Singing

When we compare the two kinds of singing, popular and classical, several important aspects come into play: range, articulation, interpretation, voice classification, quality of vibrato and intensity.

Popular singing does not demand as much range as classical singing, in which compositions explore the extremes of the voice. In popular singing, what prevails is freedom of expression, without such strict technical rigor. Thanks to the microphone, popular singers can use the voice at lower intensity and, for example, interpret a song in a personal way, varying rhythmic phrasing and improvising melodically.

Another aspect is the way the musical phrase is pronounced. In classical singing the sound comes first, and every sacrifice is made to beautify it, even at the expense of the intelligibility of the words and lyrics, which are of paramount importance in popular singing.

Genres in Popular Music

Popular music encompasses countless genres, such as samba; chorinho; frevo and baião, both from Brazil’s Northeast; maracatu; música caipira (Brazilian country music); the nativista music of Rio Grande do Sul; traditional ballads; rock; blues; and jazz. In general, these genres are tied to rhythm, to culture and to the shaping of a society.

Popular Music in the Twentieth Century

At the turn of the twentieth century, popular music took the form of light opera, or “operetta,” and of the entertainment that dominated the theaters. Fifty years later, the world had fallen under the spell of rock ’n’ roll. The catalyst for this transformation came when the descendants of enslaved West Africans gave the United States the rhythm of jazz.

In turn-of-the-century Brazil, composers in Rio de Janeiro began playing foreign rhythms in a very distinctive way. The instruments they used lent the music a melancholy tone, which gave this new style its name: choro (literally “weeping”), an intricate, largely instrumental genre. The first piece to bear the name was “Apanhei-te, Cavaquinho,” by Ernesto Nazaré. Another pioneer was Chiquinha Gonzaga, who also enjoyed enormous success with her marches and modinhas (sentimental parlor songs), including the celebrated “Abre Alas,” considered the first Carnival song written in Rio.

Musical comedy developed alongside jazz. Broadway, Manhattan’s theater district, reached its peak in the 1920s. Memorable songs captured the public imagination in lighthearted shows such as Lady, Be Good (1924), George Gershwin’s first major musical, which fused classical, popular and jazz idioms to create a new, quintessentially American sound.

In Brazil, choro was no longer purely instrumental; it was now sung as well. Influenced by maxixe and samba, it began to be played at a faster, more cheerful pace. Thus was born chorinho, or samba-choro, a style that spread through the dance halls of Rio. One of the genre’s founders was the composer Pixinguinha.

In the United States, musicals took over the Hollywood screen. Radio star Bing Crosby showed off his distinctive voice in King of Jazz (1930).

The end of Prohibition forced many musicians to trade nightlife for daylight. Jazz adapted its style, shaping itself to ballroom dancing in the form of big orchestras and swing. Bandleaders Count Basie, Duke Ellington and Paul Whiteman led the change. Billie Holiday was the great diva of the decade, with her interpretations of popular songs. In 1937, Glenn Miller came to define the era of the big bands.

The 1930s were the “golden age” of Brazilian music. Noel Rosa turned everyday situations into poetry in his sambas, as in “Com que roupa eu vou?” (“What Shall I Wear?”), from 1931. With the growth of radio came singers such as Francisco Alves and Orlando Silva, “the singer of the multitudes,” who performed sambas by Lamartine Babo, João de Barro and Ataulfo Alves. In 1938, Carmen Miranda sang Dorival Caymmi’s “O que é que a baiana tem?” (“What Does the Bahian Girl Have?”) in the film Banana da Terra. Ary Barroso wrote “Aquarela do Brasil,” recorded in 1939 and known to English-speaking audiences simply as “Brazil.”

Hollywood continued to launch American stars such as Ella Fitzgerald and Frank Sinatra. In 1942, the musical genius of Irving Berlin produced “White Christmas”; Bing Crosby’s version sold more than 30 million records. In contrast to the smoothness of swing, a new style of jazz arose: bebop, or “hot jazz,” an experimental form built on dissonant rhythms and harmonies, led by saxophonist Charlie Parker, trumpeter Dizzy Gillespie and guitarist Charlie Christian, the first to play with electric amplification.

In Brazil, baião, a genre rooted in the folklore of the Northeast, spread through the cities. The song that shares its name with the genre, released in 1946, was followed by other hits by Luiz Gonzaga, such as “Asa Branca” and “Assum Preto.” In 1947, Lupicínio Rodrigues’s “Nervos de Aço” (“Nerves of Steel”) opened a long gallery of famous sambas-canção, slow, torch-song sambas centered on the theme of dor de cotovelo, the ache of a broken heart.

The blend of Black rhythm and blues with white country music produced the style that would dominate the decades to come: rock ’n’ roll.

Samba-canção remained a staple of Brazilian radio in the 1950s, in the voices of Dalva de Oliveira, Emilinha Borba and Cauby Peixoto. In 1958, a jazz-influenced style emerged that revolutionized urban samba: bossa nova (literally “new trend”). Its leading figures were Tom Jobim and João Gilberto. Gilberto broke with the dramatic, bel canto-style delivery of the radio singers, offering sophisticated, intimate interpretations accompanied by acoustic guitar, as in “Chega de Saudade.”

In the United States, rock ’n’ roll gave way to a revival of folk and pure rhythm and blues. Singers Bob Dylan and Joan Baez led the movement. Dylan’s “Blowin’ in the Wind” became an anthem of the civil rights movement.

In Britain, the Beatles created a musical style all their own. In 1962, the single “Love Me Do” launched an unbroken run of chart-topping hits that lasted until 1970. Rock continued to evolve in Britain with the experimental guitar of Jimi Hendrix and the Rolling Stones’ mix of rhythm and blues and rock ’n’ roll. In 1968, a heavier style of rock appeared in the music of Black Sabbath.

In Brazil, the transgressive performances of Caetano Veloso, Gilberto Gil and Os Mutantes marked the birth of Tropicália at TV Record’s 1967 song festival; first prize, however, went to “Ponteio,” by Edu Lobo and Capinam. Far from the politically charged atmosphere of the festivals, young people in São Paulo were having fun with rock ’n’ roll, creating the Jovem Guarda (“Young Guard”) movement, whose leading names were Erasmo Carlos, Wanderléa and Roberto Carlos. The 1960s also saw a return to traditional samba, with recordings of songs by Cartola and Nelson Cavaquinho in the voice of Nana Caymmi.

Led Zeppelin’s style of rock became known as heavy metal, and in the 1970s new bands such as Van Halen (1974) and Def Leppard (1977) adopted the new sound. A blend of rock and melodic pop gave rise to the glam rock of Elton John and David Bowie. In 1976, the Sex Pistols assailed Britain with “Anarchy in the UK,” ushering in punk rock.

Disco, by contrast, gained international momentum, with a pop beat made for dancing and an emphasis on studio production. The elaborate vocal harmonies of the Swedish group ABBA were a hit all over Europe, with songs such as “Dancing Queen” (1976). In Jamaica, ska and rocksteady evolved into reggae, with its strong, heavy syncopated beat and deep bass line. Jamaican singer Bob Marley popularized the genre worldwide with “Get Up, Stand Up,” from 1973.

In Brazil, Milton Nascimento released the album Clube da Esquina in 1972; two years later, Elis Regina reached the peak of her career when she recorded Elis & Tom with Tom Jobim, singing classics such as “Águas de Março” (“Waters of March”). Composers devoted to instrumental music emerged, such as Egberto Gismonti and Hermeto Pascoal. In rock, Rita Lee, having parted ways with Os Mutantes, launched her solo career, and Raul Seixas became famous with the 1973 song “Ouro de Tolo” (“Fool’s Gold”).

Punk survived through the 1980s in Britain and gave rise to hardcore: fast, distorted and uncommercial. In the United States, Madonna’s debut album (1983) produced American hits such as “Holiday” and “Lucky Star.” Two other vocalists stood out: Prince and Michael Jackson, whose Thriller (1982) became the best-selling album of all time.

From the 1990s onward, popular music became even more globalized and diverse. Singers such as Whitney Houston and Mariah Carey established a heavily melismatic style of singing in pop and R&B that has influenced generations of performers, a subject we will return to in the chapter on melismas. Hip-hop and R&B came to dominate the charts; sertanejo, Brazil’s country music, took on a fresh look in the voices and arrangements of so-called sertanejo universitário (“college sertanejo”); funk carioca, the electronic dance music of Rio’s favelas, became one of the most listened-to genres in the country; and with the internet and streaming, styles from all over the world, from K-pop to trap, now sit side by side on the same playlists. For singers and voice teachers alike, this means an ever more diverse repertoire. A command of the fundamentals of technique, vocal health and ornamentation is what allows a singer to move between styles without compromising the health of the voice.

Chapter 9Breathing and Support

The Respiratory System

The respiratory system comprises the thorax, which houses the lungs. The thorax is made up of twelve pairs of ribs, attached at the back to the spine, that curve inward and slope downward. Through their cartilages, the ribs connect to the sternum at the front, with the exception of the last two pairs, the floating ribs, which give the lower part of the thorax greater mobility from front to back and from side to side. The ribs are joined to one another by inspiratory and expiratory muscles. A very powerful set of muscles covers the rib cage and makes possible the movements needed to draw air into the lungs and expel it.

The abdominal wall is made up of several muscles, chief among them the transversus abdominis, the external oblique, the internal oblique and the rectus abdominis. Together they give the respiratory system its elasticity and take part, as a whole, in the dynamics of breathing and in supporting the voice. We must also remember the latissimus dorsi, in the lower back, which is used in inhalation and, above all, in exhalation. The work of this powerful musculature is indispensable for keeping the ribs firm during singing. The activity of this group of muscles makes it possible to modulate and regulate expiratory pressure according to the demands of the music, through conscious and continuous control.

Lung capacity varies from person to person, according to age and sex. It also differs with posture: it is smaller when lying down, greatest when standing and slightly reduced when sitting. In the singing voice, the volume of air used normally ranges from 1 to 1.5 liters, depending on the length of the phrase. It also varies with the type of breathing. In upper costal breathing it is minimal, because this is the narrowest part of the rib cage: it allows little expansion and provokes untimely contractions in the neck and the laryngeal muscles. In addition, the rise of the diaphragm is limited by the absence of abdominal movement.

Breath control matters: the way breath is conserved, disciplined and consciously used to supply the pressure the music requires.

The Larynx

The larynx sits above the trachea, in front of the lower part of the pharynx, behind and below the base of the tongue, at the level of the last four cervical vertebrae. It consists of numerous cartilages linked and articulated with one another, and with neighboring structures, by ligaments and membranes. It is moved by a set of muscles that run in every direction and are covered by mucous membrane.

Some of these muscles lower or raise the larynx; others are constrictors; still others allow the vocal folds to adduct, abduct and move. The larynx is surrounded by the muscles of the neck, whose excessive contraction can obstruct or compress blood flow through the large vessels. The larynx itself has a rich blood supply.

In singing, as in speech, the larynx is a mobile organ and must be free to rise and fall with the contours of the melodic line. But it can rise, lower or shift forward and backward only through the movements of the tongue and jaw and through changes in the volume of the resonating cavities. This is how the larynx preserves its mobility and the mechanism of the vocal folds. It also changes position according to the position of the head, expiratory pressure and articulatory posture, all of which result from a physiological mechanism governed by precise movements under our voluntary control.

Through particular kinds of training, the larynx can also be made to adapt in ways other than those just described, or to adopt predetermined, automatic postures that run counter to its physiological function. If we force the larynx into a constantly low position (and therefore one that is tense throughout the entire range), or into a position that is always very high, there will be no relationship between the position of the organ and the pitch being sung. In both cases, these unphysiological positions prevent the resonating cavities from adapting, alter the vibration of the vocal folds, interfere with articulation and distort the timbre. Whenever certain basic principles are disregarded, singing soon becomes difficult.

The larynx is the vibrating element, since it houses the vocal folds. There are two of them, white in color, formed by horizontally positioned muscular folds. Their front attachment lies at the level of the Adam’s apple. Their size varies with sex, age and voice type. They are attached to cartilages that are linked to one another by the muscles that make them work, and they are covered by a mucosa set in motion by wave-like movements traveling from bottom to top and from front to back.

The vocal folds come together and move apart in movements that correspond to pitch. The higher the frequency, the higher the sound, and vice versa for low sounds. Their closure, which may be more or less deep and firm, varies in pressure and extent according to the note being sung. They can also lengthen, stretch, thin, thicken or relax.

The vocal folds produce a complex sound, made up of a fundamental, which determines pitch, and the higher harmonics that accompany it, which are multiples of that fundamental. They are set into vibration under the command of the brain and monitored by the hearing centers. At the moment of laryngeal sound production, several conditions must be met: consciously regulated pressure, appropriate shaping of the supralaryngeal cavities and good placement of resonance. Together, this muscular activity produces and sustains acoustic signals of varying frequency.

The Singer and the Instrument

Singers are their own instrument. They should not shape it to suit their personal taste; the task is simply to learn to use the voice that nature has given them. This happens only when the laws of physics, phonetics, acoustics and physiology are respected. Singers must be able to sing across their entire range, respecting the colors of the voice, with a voice that is in tune and homogeneous, in both loud and soft singing, with no change in timbre and with carrying power that comes without strain.

Singers should rely on visual feedback, which makes it easier to judge the movements of articulation, the tongue and the jaw, and so to avoid untimely tension in the face and in the breathing. This aid is complemented by tactile feedback at the abdomen, the rectus abdominis and the base of the rib cage, which simultaneously reveals faults in breathing mechanics and the quality of the work of the costo-abdominal muscles. It is also possible to feel laryngeal strain by placing the fingers on either side of the Adam’s apple, and in the same way to sense when the larynx sits too high or too low relative to its normal position.

Learning to Sing Is Learning to Breathe

The voice exists only from the moment exhaled air from the lungs supplies enough pressure to initiate and sustain the laryngeal sound. Learning to sing is, first of all, learning to breathe, that is, training to develop a way of breathing suited to singing.

Breathing is vital, because it ensures the chemical exchange between air and blood. In that role it is an unconscious, instinctive act whose movements are regular, rhythmic, symmetrical and synchronized. For the student of singing, however, breathing movements must become conscious and disciplined so that, after long training, they grow active and effective and can be placed at the service of the voice and of the music.

This training engages the nervous system and a whole set of muscles, in order to develop the agility and firmness that every muscular activity requires. Breathing for singing differs in some respects from breathing for speech, because of the demands the music imposes.

For singers, then, it is never a matter of letting the breath flow out unchecked. On the contrary, they must learn to conserve and meter it, constantly regulating how much air they spend according to the intensity, pitch, timbre, range and duration of the musical phrase.

Singers must deliberately delay the closing of the ribs, with the help of abdominal support and the strength of the paravertebral muscles. It is the costo-abdominal muscles, anchored on the spine, that regulate expiratory pressure. The muscles of the abdominal wall and of the lower rib cage find their point of resistance against this dorsolumbar musculature.

This costo-abdominal and dorsal firmness is what we call breath management. But this firmness, this muscular activity, must never be excessive. It should be balanced and measured, never give an impression of overexertion, and be ready to release at any moment. Vocal quality does not call for excessive, unbridled pressure, just as muscular strength does not call for rigid muscles; what it requires is muscle tone, the ability to engage or release at any moment, as the singer wishes.

Achieving this takes long years of work, especially at the beginning. Some people are rigid, either because they do not apply themselves to what is asked of them, because they are asked to do more than they are able to, or simply because it is in their nature. Others, by contrast, quickly acquire an agile action and sufficient capacity, and strike a balance between the work of the abdominal muscles and the opening of the ribs. For them, training will be easy and effective, all the more so if they are attentive and have an instinct for singing.

When phrases are fairly long, the breath must be carefully metered from the outset; how it is metered, however, depends on the phrase and on whether it begins low or high. In the first case (a low start), more air is usually spent, because the vocal folds vibrate more slowly and little pressure is required. Spending too much air prevents the low notes from ringing or tempts the singer to squeeze. The singer must moderate the active contraction of the abdominal wall and work to sustain the breath, extending its duration: the air should be conserved, but not held back. In the second case (a high start), air consumption is moderate and pressure is greater, since the vocal folds vibrate at a faster frequency. This greater pressure calls for more activity from the costo-abdominal muscles and more firmness in the paravertebral region. The closing of the ribs must be delayed.

Between these two extremes lie all the intermediate gradations imposed by variations in pitch, intensity, timbre and duration. At the very top of the range, one should not take in a large quantity of air. It is a common mistake to think that the more air you have, the better supported your high notes will be, but the opposite is true. This confuses capacity with pressure, that is, the quantity of air inhaled with muscle tone. Taking in more air than usual for high notes forces the larynx into exaggerated contraction and also forces the vocal folds to close too forcefully. In singing, you never need to inhale the maximum amount of air; that is reserved for certain breathing exercises.

Because all vocalises move semitone by semitone, from low to high and back again, capacity and pressure must be adjusted constantly. In other words, as you ascend, capacity tends to decrease while pressure gradually increases; the reverse happens as you descend the scale. It is always a question of metering the amount of air inhaled and the degree of tone imposed on the costo-abdominal and paravertebral muscles.

These adjustments are required by the constant changes in pitch, duration, intensity and timbre, by the position of the larynx and by the vibration frequency of the vocal folds. In legato singing, regulating the breath is even more meticulous and delicate: the flow of air must be controlled, but not held back.

When a sound grows louder, pressure must be increased steadily through a progressive contraction of the abdominal wall and the rectus abdominis. As the sound diminishes, the reserve of air begins to run out; the singer must then maintain pressure to compensate for the dwindling air, with even greater care, because the possibilities for support diminish and singers tend to let their muscles go slack. The activity is less dynamic than in louder sounds, but it must be metered at every moment. The quality of the timbre comes from the muscular work, from breath control and from keeping the place of resonance.

For sounds that need little air, the singer must supply a moderate, equally sustained pressure that builds toward the end of the phrase, especially if the phrase lies high. With well-metered pressure, the sound will keep its vocal color and remain stable. As in legato singing, however, attention must also go to preserving the shape of the supralaryngeal cavities and the place of resonance. The organs must work closely together, with constant concern for effective management of the breath.

When phrases are very intense, a large amount of air is needed, but above all good abdominal support, very low in the body, bracing against the back and paravertebral muscles. At the very top of the range, maximum pressure should never be used: you should always feel that you could give more. Muscular firmness must not be confused with strain or rigidity.

If a sound begins on a vowel, we should not immediately apply all the pressure the note requires, even if it is a high note that calls for a great deal of pressure. You must “step into the place of the sound” with agility and precision. Immediately afterward, pressure should be increased through active, progressive abdominal contraction. This must happen so quickly that no difference in timbre or intonation can be heard.

At the moment of onset, in addition to regulating the breath, the singer must have prepared in advance the resonating organs through which the sound will spread, and must locate in the mind the place where the sound will vibrate. You can recognize a good singer by the way he or she begins a note. Some start with a hard glottal attack; others scoop up to the note from below. Both habits are to be avoided: the first because it traumatizes the vocal folds, the second because it reveals a lack of synchrony between breath pressure and vocal fold vibration.

To carry out this breathing technique, the muscles of the chest and abdomen must be able to supply more or less tone at the singer’s will, even within the span of a single note or musical phrase. Sustaining the breath therefore means being able to adjust pressure at any moment. Controlling the respiratory muscles makes the natural movements of the larynx and of articulation easier and allows the resonating cavities to adapt. The singing voice demands continuous muscular activity, constantly adjusted to every situation. Singers must pay attention to the work of their muscles and remain conscious of their breathing technique, on which the quality of their sound depends.

It takes many months before singers can feel and carry out what is asked of them. Beginners who sing easily often see no need to worry about their breath; they would rather “listen to themselves.” When singers trust their own facility and ignore the importance of breathing, serious difficulties may soon appear, such as loss of the high notes, trouble with register transitions and voice breaks, which may in turn be followed by vocal disorders such as nodules.

Given how fragile the voice is, singers must have sound technique, since the voice is not controlled by hearing alone. For that reason, even when they produce beautiful sounds, they need to know what those qualities are owed to. They must also be able to deal with temporary setbacks such as fatigue. In that case, it is better to sing with less intensity and compensate with more resonance, that is, by making the fullest possible use of the resonating cavities. Even for the most gifted, then, regular breathing work aims to train the muscles so that they acquire the agility and tone that every muscular activity requires. Turning an instinctive act into a disciplined, mastered one will allow singers to meet any difficulty they encounter.

Types of Breathing

The literature describes four basic types of breathing:

  1. Clavicular, or upper;
  2. Middle, mixed or thoracic (chest);
  3. Lower, or abdominal;
  4. Complete: diaphragmatic-abdominal, or costo-diaphragmatic-abdominal.

1. Clavicular or Upper Breathing

Clavicular, or upper, breathing is characterized by expansion of the upper rib cage only, which causes a visible lifting of the shoulders and may or may not be accompanied by a forward thrust of the neck. Because lifting the rib cage recruits the neck muscles, one observes contraction of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (which acts as an accessory muscle of inhalation only when one wants to produce a very loud sound) and laryngeal tension, with a narrowing of the thyrohyoid membrane space.

Voice production suffers from the insufficient supply of air, and the resulting sound tends to be high-pitched because the larynx is raised and tense. The sternocleidomastoid muscle is often tense in dysphonic singers, since they tend to keep the rib cage constantly raised.

This kind of breathing focuses on the upper chest. It is not well suited to singing, because air fills mainly the upper portion of the lungs, which is their smallest part; it can also cause dizziness and tingling. As the chest rises, the lower ribs do not open, only the upper ones. These upper ribs are smaller and can open only slightly, because they have little room to expand.

As a result, the singer will have little breath, and unwanted constriction may occur around the vocal folds. It is also hard to keep the spine upright with this type of breathing. It occurs mainly in situations of tension, such as when we are running or frightened, and it is therefore unsuitable for singing, which requires a relaxed neck and relaxed articulators.

2. Middle, Mixed or Thoracic Breathing

Middle, mixed or thoracic breathing, the type most commonly observed in the general population, shows little upper or lower movement during inhalation and a forward displacement of the middle chest. It is the breathing we use for most of the day, at rest or in casual conversation, but it is inadequate and insufficient for professional voice use, and especially for singing.

3. Lower or Abdominal Breathing

Lower, or abdominal, breathing is characterized by an absence of movement in the upper region, which is usually underdeveloped, with the shoulders rolled forward, and by expansion of the lower region. It appears in people with little energy, in whom a sense of the chest collapsing is quite evident, but it may also have been cultivated as the “correct way to breathe” as a result of misguided instruction.

This is the breathing used by the German school of classical singing, in which you push the belly outward. This type of breathing pulls the trachea downward, because the diaphragm descends excessively, and consequently leaves the larynx with little mobility. Because the larynx is held very low, the sound becomes darker and the high notes harder to reach. This type of breathing can also make you hunch your back, which makes it difficult to open the lower ribs, the largest ones, and fails to create enough room for the lungs to work freely, pushing the belly outward instead.

The body’s balance is reduced, and there is no reason to make this movement, since the lungs are located in the chest, not in the abdomen. If you are a singer who dances, jumps or moves around a great deal on stage, your mobility may be compromised; if your style calls for the extremes of the voice, you will probably be unable to produce healthy distortion. This technique is an old one, and advances in scientific research have shown us more effective ways to breathe for singing. Moreover, with prolonged use of this technique, the belly becomes somewhat slack and grows used to protruding.

4. Diaphragmatic-Abdominal or Costo-Diaphragmatic-Abdominal Breathing

Diaphragmatic-abdominal, or costo-diaphragmatic-abdominal, breathing, also called complete or total breathing, is characterized by a harmonious expansion of the entire rib cage, without excess in either the upper or the lower region. The whole lung area is put to use, and it is the most mechanically efficient form of breathing for developing a professional voice. The greater the vocal demand, the deeper complete breathing becomes.

We can therefore conclude that middle, or thoracic, breathing is sufficient for everyday voice use and, in fact, represents the most economical breathing mechanism in terms of energy expenditure.

It is demanding work, because it runs counter to the body’s natural movement: when we exhale, the ribs tend to close. Keeping the ribs open in this way creates ample space in the rib cage and lets the lungs work freely, with nothing pressing on them to force the air out.

You are the one in control of your column of air. When you open your ribs, pay close attention to the movement. Do not let your shoulders and chest rise. You will learn to work with the intercostal muscles, which are under voluntary control. That means you can open the ribs alone, without moving anything else, such as the shoulders or neck. It is like opening your hand without moving your arm.

Tip

Always practice in front of a mirror so that you can see your overall posture, and avoid tension in the shoulders and neck.

Support

To sing well and without breaks in the voice, we must use a form of support based on the entire abdominal musculature, including the rectus abdominis, the obliques at the sides, the lower abdominal muscles, the latissimus dorsi and the other back muscles, as well as the pelvic floor. We call this support. Support works like a piston that pushes the column of air out of the lungs.

When we use support (the contraction of the abdominal muscles), we push the abdominal organs upward; they in turn push the diaphragm, which pushes the column of air. In this way, the effort is taken away from the neck and shifted to the abdominal wall. The more open your ribs are, the better your support will work.

Good support calls for precision in the use of your energy and physical strength. It is therefore variable: do not overuse it when it is not needed. Some musical genres do not require very strong support, so do not waste your energy for nothing. We will use two terms to describe the variations in air pressure produced by the abdominal muscles:

  • Support: the act of sustaining. You hold the muscles that keep the ribs open, preventing air from escaping unnecessarily. This keeps the air pressure even as you sing and safeguards the quality of the sound.
  • Counter-support: pushing the air. Here you use the abdominal and back muscles to control the pressure of the air column according to vocal needs, such as volume, pitch (low notes call for less pressure, high notes for more), added effects and so on. The abdominal contraction is more vigorous.

Support is made up of several muscle groups working together:

  • the rectus abdominis and the lateral obliques;
  • the pelvic floor;
  • a complex of muscles in the pelvic region, including the glutes;
  • the latissimus dorsi.

Support Exercises

The Starting Position

All the exercises that follow begin from the same position: standing with your back against a wall, feet hip-width apart, knees soft, spine aligned, shoulders relaxed, chin vertically aligned with your feet and ribs open. Whenever the text asks you to “take the Starting Position,” return to this posture before you begin.

Level 1 · Sustaining

All exercises should be done with your back against the wall.

Take the Starting Position. Inhale, then release the air on a long, gentle “ssss,” conserving it for as long as you can. It is worth timing yourself so you can track your progress over the course of your training.

Keep your ribs open: that way you will not compress your lungs or lose air needlessly. Believe me, you may need a reserve of air for longer phrases. Use the rectus abdominis to control the air pressure. The “ssss” should be continuous, with no variations in intensity. Gradually draw your belly inward.

When you are running out of air, it is time for the insider’s trick. To squeeze out a little more air in songs with extremely long phrases, gradually close your ribs while pulling your belly in and up. This compresses the lungs from the bottom up and lets you use your air to the very end.

Level 1 · Strength: The Four Supports

  1. Take the Starting Position. Inhale and release all your air in five seconds on a very strong “ssss,” pulling your belly in. Notice the pressure produced.
  2. Take the Starting Position. Inhale and release all your air in five seconds on a very strong “ssss,” pulling your belly in and pushing your sides out. To feel the side muscles working, press your hands against your waist, just above the hip bones. Cough lightly or laugh heartily: you will feel the muscles move outward. Notice the increase in pressure compared with the previous exercise.
  3. Take the Starting Position. Inhale and release all your air in five seconds on a very strong “ssss,” pulling your belly in, pushing your sides out and adding pressure from the pelvic floor. Squeeze your glutes. Notice the increase in pressure compared with the previous exercises.
  4. Take the Starting Position. Inhale and release all your air in five seconds on a very strong “ssss,” pulling your belly in, pushing your sides out, engaging the pelvic floor and adding the strength of your limbs to activate the latissimus dorsi. Place your palms together and press them as if you were crushing something between them. Be careful at this point: do not tense your shoulders or neck; the effort stays in the support muscles alone. You can use the strength of your arms and legs to increase the pressure of your support.

Choose the posture that best suits your style and musical genre, the one that feels most comfortable, lets you produce the pressure you want and keeps tension from traveling to the upper body. Notice the increase in pressure compared with the previous exercises.

As you will have noticed, each muscle group you add increases the air pressure produced, so you need to learn when and how to use each point of support. The level of pressure depends on the intensity you want, the technique you are applying, the pitch of the note (low and high notes call for different pressures) and the interpretation.

Now that you can control your four supports, practice using them all at once, making sure that no tension is building in your neck. Hold all four supports at the same time, at full strength, and relax your neck. Turn your head to the left and then to the right as you release the air on “ssss,” aiming to keep a good level of pressure in the lower body and complete relaxation in the upper body.

Level 1 · Agility

For register transitions, note onsets and fast scales.

  1. Take the Starting Position. Practice the consonants /k/ and /g/ in staccato (a way of singing in small pulses that produces short, detached sounds separated by brief silences), throwing punches into the air and keeping your ribs open. You will feel the muscles at the pit of the stomach move outward and the lower abdominal muscles move inward. This exercise develops the correct support for phrases that require agility. The abdomen “follows” each note, producing pulses of pressure that sharpen the definition of each sound. Practice for at least five minutes.
  2. Take the Starting Position. Inhale and release the air on a staccato “s-s-s-s,” using the support you learned in the previous exercise. Watch out for hard glottal attacks. If you find it difficult, switch the “s” to an “f.” Start with one pulse on “s” or “f” per second and, once you are ready, gradually increase the number of pulses per second: two per second, three per second, four per second and so on.

General Guidelines

  • Always maintain good posture: spine straight, shoulders relaxed (never puff out your chest or raise your shoulders), chin aligned with your feet.
  • Continuously observe the behavior of your breathing muscles (abdominal and intercostal).

Chapter 10The Physiology of Phonation

What Is the Voice?

Could you say what the voice is? The voice is a sound produced by a complex neurophysiological process. It identifies a person by age, sex, physical characteristics and personality. It also reveals the person’s emotional state, and it allows us to communicate.

Where Is the Voice Produced?

The voice is produced in the larynx, a cylindrical organ located in the neck at the level of the sixth and seventh cervical vertebrae, connected below to the trachea and above to the pharynx, what we commonly call the throat. It is made up of cartilage, muscle, membrane and mucosa, and is divided into three portions: the supraglottis, the glottis and the subglottis.

The supraglottis is formed by the structures above the glottis. The glottis is the space between the vocal folds, where the voice is produced and where it immediately begins to gain resonance within the supraglottis itself. The subglottis is the portion below the glottis.

The larynx has three basic functions: breathing, swallowing and phonation. The last of these is responsible for producing the voice.

Theories of Voice Production

Several theories have been developed to explain how the voice is produced: the vibrating string theory, the mucoundulatory theory, the negative resistance theory, the myoelastic theory, the aerodynamic theory and the myoelastic-aerodynamic theory, which remains the most widely accepted to this day.

This theory describes voice production in terms of the Bernoulli effect: the increase in air velocity as it passes through the larynx draws the vocal folds together, and the elastic action of the muscles makes them spring back apart. Although it is the most widely accepted theory, it does not account for the full diversity of the human voice.

So how is the voice actually produced? None of the theories of phonation can fully explain such complexity. In what follows, we will try to explain what is known to date about vocal physiology. We know that the voice results from the amplification of phonation in the resonating cavities: the larynx itself, the pharynx, the mouth and the nose.

Phonation itself comes from the air exhaled from the lungs during breathing. As this air passes through the vocal folds, it causes them to come together and vibrate, producing a quiet, weak sound that is subsequently amplified. During inhalation and exhalation without phonation, the vocal folds remain apart.

Phonation is far more complex than air passing through and setting the vocal folds into vibration. It is a volitional act, and therefore a movement controlled by the central nervous system. It begins with a stimulus from the cerebral cortex that carries motor information to the larynx, where the movements that produce the voice are carried out. When the nerve impulse reaches the larynx, it activates the lateral cricoarytenoid and arytenoid muscles, which are laryngeal adductors that bring the vocal folds together. Abduction, the process of opening the vocal folds, is carried out by the posterior cricoarytenoid muscles.

The Glottic Cycle

At the same time as the adductor muscles act, the velocity of the exhaled air also increases. As this air passes between the vocal folds, it lowers the pressure in the glottal region, so that the mucosa of the vocal folds is literally sucked inward, bringing the fold on one side into contact with the fold on the other. Because subglottal pressure becomes greater than glottal pressure, the mucosa begins to vibrate. This process is called the glottic cycle, and it has four phases:

  1. Closed phase
  2. Opening phase
  3. Open phase
  4. Closing phase

These cycles occur at very high speed and cannot be observed individually without stroboscopic equipment.

Vocal Frequency

The frequency of vibration is measured in hertz, the number of cycles per second. The higher the frequency, the higher the sound; the lower the frequency, the lower the sound.

Do you know why women’s voices differ from men’s? The difference becomes more pronounced during adolescence, through a process called voice change, or mutation, in which changes in the larynx directly affect the characteristics of a person’s voice. These changes are driven by new hormone levels and cause the larynx to grow front to back much more markedly in males than in females. Evidence of this is the protrusion of the thyroid cartilage popularly known as the Adam’s apple.

In males, too, the larynx descends further in the neck and the mass of the vocal folds increases, since the greater the mass and length of the vocal folds, the lower the larynx sits and the deeper the voice. The male voice will therefore be lower than the female voice (at least in the most typical cases).

Tension and Elongation

Factors such as the mass, tension and elongation of the vocal folds can lower or raise the frequency of vibration. The last of these, elongation, is chiefly responsible for the range of phonatory frequencies within a single individual; in other words, it is how we are able to make our voice lower or higher.

The cricothyroid muscle draws the thyroid and cricoid cartilages toward each other, lengthening the vocal folds and producing higher sounds, in a process known as the cricothyroid tilt (the “báscula” movement). The thyroarytenoid muscle, by contrast, shortens the vocal folds, producing lower sounds.

Vocal Intensity

How do we make our voice louder or softer? Vocal intensity is linked to the resistance the glottis offers to the airflow. The greater this resistance, the greater the subglottal pressure and the greater the force of vibration, and therefore the louder the sound. The weaker the resistance, the lower the intensity.

Vocal Ontogeny

Why does the voice change over time? The voice develops throughout every stage of life, undergoing changes that accompany a person’s physical, psychological and social development. Changes in the size of the larynx, in the thickness and length of the vocal folds and in the characteristics of the laryngeal mucosa and muscles give rise to different vocal configurations over the course of a lifetime. This process is called vocal ontogeny.

The Organs of the Vocal and Respiratory Systems

Producing sound involves the movements of the speech organs. To understand the structure and anatomy, as well as the function and physiology, of each organ, and the role each one plays in producing sound, we need to study the vocal apparatus. The so-called phonatory organs serve primarily for breathing, chewing and swallowing; their secondary function is to produce the sounds of human speech.

Lungs
Air is the basic ingredient of speech: it supplies the energy needed for phonation. The airstream used in phonation is set in motion by the walls of the lungs, the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm. Air leaves the lungs, passes through the larynx and exits through the mouth, the nose or both. Speech is normally produced during exhalation, but some sounds are produced during inhalation.

Did you know? On average, the lungs weigh about 700 grams (roughly 1.5 pounds) and measure 25 cm (about 10 inches) in height.
Diaphragm
A broad skeletal (striated) muscle that separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity. It is attached to the lumbar vertebrae, the lower ribs and the sternum. It has three main openings, which allow the passage of the esophagus, nerves, the aorta, lymphatic vessels and the vessels of the thorax. In humans, this muscle has a ridged appearance and faces upward, with its front part higher than its back part.

At rest, the diaphragm is dome-shaped. During inhalation, the muscle contracts and flattens, increasing the volume of the thorax; in the process, air flows into the lungs to fill the resulting vacuum. When the muscle relaxes, the accumulated air is expelled. The diaphragm also plays an important role in digestion: when it contracts, it exerts pressure on the abdomen. Its movements are likewise important for coughing, sneezing, childbirth and defecation.
Larynx
A short organ that connects the pharynx to the trachea. It lies on the midline of the neck, in front of the fourth, fifth and sixth cervical vertebrae. The wall of the larynx is composed of nine cartilages: three unpaired and three paired. The three unpaired cartilages are the thyroid cartilage, the epiglottis and the cricoid cartilage.

The thyroid cartilage consists of hyaline cartilage and forms the front and side walls of the larynx; it is larger in men because of the influence of hormones during puberty. The back edges of its plates bear short, thick, peg-like extensions called the superior and inferior horns. The cricoid cartilage lies just below the thyroid cartilage, directly above the trachea. The epiglottis is attached to the hyoid bone and the thyroid cartilage; it acts as a kind of “door” to the lungs, through which only air and other gases pass in and out. Liquids and solids do not enter the lungs, because the epiglottis closes and directs them to the esophagus.

The paired cartilages are the arytenoid, corniculate and cuneiform cartilages. The arytenoid cartilage articulates with the cricoid, forming a synovial (diarthrodial) joint. The corniculate cartilage sits atop the arytenoid. The cuneiform cartilage is very small and lies in front of its corresponding corniculate cartilage, connecting each arytenoid to the epiglottis.

The larynx also plays a role in sound production, which results in phonation. On its inner surface is a front-to-back cleft called the laryngeal vestibule, which contains two pairs of folds: the vestibular folds (false vocal folds) and the vocal folds (true vocal folds).
Vocal folds
Two elastic membranes positioned horizontally in the larynx, like a pair of lips, running from the Adam’s apple at the front of the larynx (the anterior part) to the arytenoid cartilages at the back (the posterior part). At the front, the vocal folds are attached to the thyroid cartilage, and at the back to the two arytenoid cartilages. Guided by the arytenoids, they can come together, blocking the passage of air, or move apart, letting air pass freely.

The ends of the vocal folds attached to the thyroid always remain together; only the ends attached to the arytenoids move, and it is they that set the vocal folds in motion. The opening between the folds is the glottis, which, when open, is V-shaped.
Epiglottis
A cartilage located at the top of the larynx that acts as its lid or valve, preventing food from entering the trachea and, consequently, the lungs. During phonation and breathing, it opens the entrance to the larynx, leaving the airway clear.
Pharynx
The cavity located between the mouth (the root of the tongue), the nasal passages, the larynx and the esophagus. Air coming from the lungs through the larynx passes through the pharynx and exits through the mouth, the nose or both, depending on the position of the soft palate. The pharynx can change shape and size when the root of the tongue retracts, when the larynx moves up or down, or when the back wall of the pharynx contracts during phonation and swallowing. It acts as a resonator, shaping the resonance of vowel sounds.

The upper portion of the pharynx, called the nasal part or nasopharynx, communicates with the choanae, with the pharyngeal openings of the auditory (Eustachian) tubes and with the oropharynx. The oropharynx communicates with the mouth and serves as a passage for both air and food. The laryngopharynx extends downward from the hyoid bone and connects with the esophagus (the food passage) behind and with the larynx (the air passage) in front; like the oral part of the pharynx, it is both an airway and a digestive pathway. The auditory tube also communicates with the pharynx through its pharyngeal opening, which in turn connects the nasal part of the pharynx with the tympanic cavity of the middle ear.
Uvula
The extension of the soft palate. It is set into vibration when we articulate uvular trills.
Palate
The roof of the mouth. The front part (two-thirds of the palate), bony and fixed, is called the hard palate, or palatal vault. The back part, muscular and mobile, is called the soft palate, or velum. The soft palate can lower, letting air escape through the nasal cavity (as in breathing or in producing nasal sounds). When it is raised and pressed against the back wall of the pharynx, it closes off the nasal passage and air exits through the mouth (as in speaking oral sounds). It can also be partly raised, letting air pass through the mouth and the nose at the same time (as in articulating nasalized sounds).
Tongue
The principal organ of articulation. It finds points of contact on different parts of the palatal vault and can move its tip toward the lower or upper teeth as well as raise its base toward the back of the palate. Through the muscles that connect it to the surrounding structures, it takes part in opening the mouth, in the movements of the soft palate and in the tone and firmness of articulation.

Because all the muscles of this region are directly interconnected, and their fibers run continuously into the muscles of the hypopharynx and larynx, any downward and backward contraction will trigger action in other muscles of the region, whereas a normal position of the base of the tongue eases the contractions of this whole group of muscles. Likewise, any action that seeks to widen the resonating cavities from side to side will tend to ease the action of the muscles that close or narrow the opening of the hypopharynx and the back of the mouth.
Teeth
The teeth serve as a point of articulation. Set in the alveolar ridge, they can partly or completely block the passage of air when they come into contact with the lips or the tip of the tongue.
Nose
A protrusion at the center of the face. Its outer part is called the external nose, and the hollow within it is known as the nasal cavity. The external nose is shaped like a three-sided pyramid with its base at the bottom, whose back face fits vertically against the middle third of the face. The sides of the nose each have a crescent-shaped flare called the ala, or wing, of the nose. The nasal cavity is divided into two compartments, one right and one left.

Each compartment has a front opening, the nostril, and a back opening, called the choana. The choanae connect the nasal cavity to the pharynx. It is in the nasal cavity that air is conditioned, that is, filtered, humidified and warmed. On the side wall of the nasal cavity are the nasal conchae (turbinates), divided into superior, middle and inferior.
Trachea
A tube that continues on from the larynx, enters the thorax and ends by dividing into the two main bronchi. It runs along the midline, in front of the esophagus, veering slightly to the right only at its lower end. The framework of the trachea consists of roughly twenty C-shaped rings of cartilage, open at the back, called the tracheal cartilages.
Lips
The lips shape the articulation of contoids (consonant sounds) and vocoids (vowel sounds). In contoids, the lips can partly or completely block the passage of air, as in [f], [p] and [m]. In vocoids, the degree of lip rounding and protrusion distinguishes one sound from another, as in [i] and [ü]. In general, the higher the tongue in the articulation of a rounded vocoid, the more rounded and protruded the lips: in [u], the tongue is high and the lips are more rounded and protruded; in [i], the tongue is at medium height and the lips are less rounded and protruded. The degree to which the lips project forward, like the degree to which the mouth opens, enlarges the resonating chamber and so affects the timbre of the sounds.
Lower jaw (mandible)
The jaw determines how far the mouth opens or closes and the distance between the tongue and the palate, distinguishing high vocoids from low ones and enlarging or reducing the size of the oral cavity.
Resonating chambers
They reinforce and intensify the vibrations (the sounds) produced by the vocal folds. The quality of the sound depends on the shape and volume of the resonator. The resonators are the pharyngeal cavity (pharynx), the oral cavity (mouth), the nasal cavity (nasal passages) and the labial cavity (lips projected forward).

Chapter 11Exploring Your Own Voice

When the moment comes to sing, singers must prepare themselves physically and mentally. They need to concentrate and form an acoustic image of the sound: its pitch, its color as the ear perceives it, its mechanism and the sensations it produces. This motor and sensory command, which originates in the nerve centers, sets in motion the various movements of the organs and the work of the muscles.

In practical terms, this means the singer assumes a phonatory posture, which requires control of the breathing action and of the amount of air inhaled for the chosen exercise. It is a conscious and indispensable preparation on which vocal production depends: the voluntary shaping of the resonating cavities, the widening of the faucial pillars together with the lifting of the soft palate, and the work of the pharyngeal wall. Checking the articulatory positions of the tongue and lips for each sound pronounced is essential if the larynx is to carry out its natural movements freely.

From the moment singing begins, a shape, a mold, must be prepared, starting with the low notes. But as the exercises rise semitone by semitone, there is no sudden change in technique: there is continuity, a progression at every level, in expiratory pressure and in the increasing volume of the supralaryngeal cavities, as well as in the movement of the larynx and of the place of resonance, which shift in the same direction.

From one note to the next there is a mode of transmission that is difficult to achieve, one that evolves little by little and must be held in the mind, since this is the only way for the muscular work and the posture of the organs to change progressively with each note. As one descends the scale again, the larynx normally lowers gradually, accompanied by a relaxation of the muscles. If this relaxation is excessive, the voice drops, loses its ring and goes out of tune. To prevent this, singers must maintain the pressure, the way the breath is distributed and the place of resonance. A certain energy must be preserved even as the scale descends, and the upward direction of the breath must not be lost.

It is important to remember that, moving from low to high, the mouth opens progressively, with the jaws separating at the joint where they articulate, together with a diffuse sensation of laryngeal tension and a slight rise in the base of the tongue. As the sound rises, the exact color of the vowel is often lost, because the singer, trying to round out the sound or “yawn” it, has altered the position inside the mouth that corresponds to the vowel being sung. If, on the contrary, that position is respected, the singer obtains the characteristic vowel timbre of each vowel, corresponding to its specific formant regions. Thanks to technique, the extravocalic timbre, the color unique to each of us, is achieved. The different formants are distributed in such a way that, as it leaves the lips, the voice has an acoustic profile that is truly the singer’s own. It is generally accepted that the singing voice involves several formants.

Singers must become aware of the importance of the basic principles of vocal technique and make use of feedback from their own voice, since the voice is the product of learned behavior, developed through specific muscular activity and through the different sensations on which this technique rests. It is through progressive, well-guided training that beginners will discover and develop their sensations and learn to master the tone and agility of their muscles. They must also understand that, even in moments of great intensity, they must always be able to do more and remain in command of their muscles.

According to this technique, there must be cohesion, a continuity between the two extremes: the sensation of breath support and the sensation of vibratory tremor. The vocal and respiratory organs are joined to one another in a harmonious, balanced way, never giving the impression of effort concentrated here or there, apart from a very diffuse sensation of taking up the phonatory posture and a well-distributed effort, more or less sustained or firm according to the demands of the music. This set of movements and sensations, sought out systematically, will become, after long training, a global sense of the movements and sensations that make up the technique of the singing voice, and at the same time an effective means of control, valid for every voice.

None of this is easy to achieve. Most of the time it takes long years of progressive work and personal exploration. For students, the point is not to imitate the teacher’s voice or to chase a particular timbre! The point is simply to learn that every vocal flaw, and every success, must find its meaning in an awareness of the physical action and in control of the sound. In this way, by using sound technique, students will develop their true voice.

Inappropriate Vocal Behaviors and Habits

  • Lack of proper postural alignment between head and body;
  • Reduced body movement, with the body held still or moving only minimally while speaking or singing;
  • Letting the air out before speaking or singing;
  • Taking a deep breath before every utterance;
  • Speaking while inhaling;
  • Lack of proper coordination between breathing and phonation;
  • Keeping the shoulders and chest constantly raised while speaking or singing;
  • Generalized tension throughout the body while speaking;
  • Generalized tension in the muscles of the vocal apparatus while speaking or singing;
  • Visible contraction of the paralaryngeal muscles while speaking or singing;
  • Constant hyperextension of the neck;
  • Habitually using excessively high intensity;
  • Habitually using excessively low intensity;
  • Habitually speaking too fast;
  • Regularly and actively cheering at sporting events or playing sports that involve using the voice;
  • Singing for long periods without proper technique;
  • Modulating the voice solely by increasing intensity;
  • Fixing the voice on a single pitch;
  • Overusing very high pitches;
  • Overusing very low pitches;
  • Failing to articulate speech sounds distinctly;
  • Shifting the jaw sideways instead of opening it;
  • Habitually using hard vocal onsets;
  • Habitually using breathy vocal onsets;
  • Maintaining a constant nasal resonance focus;
  • Maintaining a constantly low resonance focus;
  • Failing to make use of resonance, resulting in overall hyporesonance;
  • Overusing a commanding, authoritative voice;
  • Habitually using a whispery vocal quality;
  • Habitually using a tense, pressed vocal quality;
  • Habitually using the basal register (vocal fry) in everyday speech;
  • Habitually engaging in abusive vocal practices, such as forceful throat clearing and coughing;
  • Skipping vocal warm-ups and cool-downs;
  • Talking over background noise;
  • Wearing clothing that is tight around the larynx or the abdomen;
  • Habitually imitating harmful vocal models, other voices, animals or noisy objects;
  • Talking for long periods with the phone receiver cradled on the shoulder;
  • Using a cell phone excessively and/or for long periods;
  • Using speakerphone excessively and/or for long periods;
  • Not drinking enough water.

Anatomical and Functional Patterns

  • Varying intensity by squeezing the laryngeal and paralaryngeal muscles;
  • Varying pitch solely by changing the vertical position of the larynx;
  • Varying pitch by compressing the glottal and supraglottal structures;
  • Limiting jaw movement while speaking;
  • Keeping the tongue constantly pushed forward while speaking;
  • Keeping the tongue constantly pulled back while speaking;
  • Keeping the tongue between the teeth (atypical swallowing, or tongue thrust);
  • Holding the larynx in a constantly high position in the neck;
  • Holding the larynx in a constantly low position in the neck;
  • Using medial (side-to-side) constriction of the laryngeal vestibule to phonate;
  • Using excessive front-to-back constriction of the laryngeal vestibule;
  • Bulging veins in the neck while speaking.

What to Avoid

  • Shouting, or talking for long stretches of time.
  • Exposure to sudden changes in temperature, including ice-cold drinks, which can cause an abrupt temperature shift (thermal shock) and trigger a release of mucus. Before swallowing anything ice-cold, hold it in your mouth for a few seconds.
  • Alcoholic beverages before singing or heavy speaking, since alcohol acts as an anesthetic: it seems to improve the voice while masking its abuse. The same is true of sprays, lozenges and mint, peppermint or ginger drops.
  • Throat clearing or coughing, since these habits create friction between the vocal folds.
  • Chocolate drinks and dairy products, especially before professional voice use, since they increase secretions in the vocal tract and lead to excessive throat clearing.
  • Overusing the voice in the premenstrual period: during this time a woman’s body swells with retained water, and so do the vocal folds, which become heavier and vibrate less easily.
  • Exposure to dust, fumes and very strong odors, especially if you have allergies.
  • Talking while exercising: during breathing the vocal folds stay open, and during speech they close in order to vibrate. Talking while breathing hard, as physical exercise demands, goes against the physiological nature of the vocal apparatus.

Recommendations

  • The pillars of singing (breathing, the use of the resonators and articulation) should at first be the focus of slow, progressive work.
  • In the beginning, the best way to build agility is to practice slowly.
  • Seek “quality” of voice first; never “force” the voice to work, and always stay relaxed when you sing.
  • The body of an instrument does not tense up when it plays. Your body should be loose and free, so avoid muscular tension.
  • Studying music, reading music well and being able to sight-sing should come before the study of singing.
  • If you have a heavy voice, work extensively with detached, staccato sounds and agility exercises. If you have a light voice, work with legato lines and sustained notes.
  • At the first sign of a cold, drink orange juice or lemonade, take vitamins and rest. Avoid needless coughing.
  • If you feel the need to cough, firm your lips, breathe calmly and swallow.
  • Always sing with a straight line in mind, so that the voice does not change “color.”
  • Do not sleep with the window open unless you are sure you will sleep with your mouth closed.
