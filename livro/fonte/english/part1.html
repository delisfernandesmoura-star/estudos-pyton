Part I

Vocal Health

Assessment, warm-up, posture, vocal hygiene, and the factors that protect or threaten the voice.

Chapter 1Speech-Language and Otolaryngological Assessment

Of all voice professionals, singers place the greatest demands on their voices. Even the slightest vocal problem is enough to make a singer tense and anxious: however minor it may be, it will have a major impact on their singing, potentially leading to vocal strain and hoarseness. For this reason, singers tend to be the group of professionals most focused on prevention and voice care.

At the first sign of a symptom, a singer should seek professional help. Sometimes the first stop is a speech-language pathologist; at other times it is a general physician or an otolaryngologist (ENT). The most cautious singers see both professionals regularly, even when nothing is wrong, in order to prevent injury and improve their vocal conditioning.

Assessment of the singing voice essentially comprises the singer's case history, which gathers important, relevant information for the diagnostic workup and subsequent treatment, together with a clinical examination by an otolaryngologist to evaluate the larynx and its respiratory, sphincteric, and phonatory functions.

The otolaryngology consultation and the laryngeal examination are inseparable from a detailed case history. The history reveals factors that can alter the voice on their own, that may also aggravate other conditions and slow the patient's recovery, and that may call for specific therapeutic intervention. Likewise, it is necessary to understand the patient's overall clinical picture and the possible physical and psychological causes that can affect the larynx and the voice.

It is essential to analyze factors specific to the vocal apparatus alongside general health conditions, such as: diabetes mellitus; high blood pressure; reduced pulmonary ventilatory capacity; heart failure; metabolic, endocrine, allergic, immunological, autoimmune, neurological, infectious, inflammatory, and digestive disorders, among others; benign or malignant tumors; the use or abuse of substances such as alcohol, caffeine, tobacco, tranquilizers, marijuana, cocaine, and others; the ongoing or prolonged use of medications such as corticosteroids, anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, and chemotherapy drugs; and states such as anxiety, depression, fatigue, stress, burnout, insomnia, pregnancy, and premenstrual syndrome.

Both the physician and the speech-language pathologist should consider the singer's vocal characteristics and complaints separately as they relate to speaking and to singing.

Although a single organ and essentially the same group of muscles are responsible for both functions, several fundamental aspects of voice production differ greatly between the speaking voice and the singing voice. In many cases, singers need a therapist's or physician's help to pinpoint their complaint, distinguishing the characteristics of the speaking voice from those of the singing voice. It is common for someone to see a professional with a complaint specific to the singing voice, such as difficulty with a register transition in a scale, or, conversely, to have no awareness at all of how they use their speaking voice.

For the speech-language pathologist who works with singers

Assessing a singer goes beyond assessing the speaking voice. It includes the singer's vocal history (style, repertoire, hours spent in rehearsals and performances, use of microphones and stage monitors), the complaint as it appears in real performance settings, and observation of the singing voice across different registers. Each of the factors listed below can be explored during the consultation and monitored over time.

Key factors in assessing the singing voice

  • Hearing disorders. Any hearing impairment can compromise the singer's entire feedback loop, the ability to hear what they are singing. Not only the voice but also hearing should be monitored, through specific tests ordered by the physician or the speech-language pathologist.
  • Types of voice disorders.
  • Anatomical variations of the head and neck. These may lead to changes in resonance and posture, and to compensatory movements during phonation.
  • Neck sensitivity. This can cause vocal tension, poor posture, and a lack of harmony between body and voice.
  • Posture. During spontaneous conversation, check the tilt of the head, tension in the neck muscles, the position of the chin relative to the chest, and the slope of the shoulders. Observe the curvature of the spine while the singer walks and sits, and determine whether the posture they adopt is suitable for phonation. It is also important to see whether the postural traits observed during speech change or remain the same during singing. Many people raise their chin when singing higher notes, in the mistaken and harmful belief that this helps the larynx rise. In fact, a larynx that rises freely, without tension and without the involvement of the extrinsic muscles, is what would genuinely help in producing high notes.

Chapter 2Vocal Warm-Up and Cool-Down

Exercising your voice safely

Caution

  • No singing exercise should ever be uncomfortable. If you feel any tension, pain, burning, or discomfort, stop immediately. Always practice carefully and attentively so that you notice any of these signals while you work. Do not push through: you could injure yourself. Stop, then start again to recover the correct posture or position for the exercise.
  • Avoid constriction. Notice whether you are jutting or clenching your jaw, or whether there is any constriction in your lips, throat, neck, and/or shoulders. Do not let a lack of breath support lock up your muscles. Strengthen your support and counter-support, and relax your upper body.

Vocal warm-up

A vocal warm-up prepares the body to perform its functions properly and helps prevent injury by reducing friction between the vocal folds and tears in the muscle fibers and connective tissue. Warm-up exercises increase blood flow, which supports greater intensity, vocal projection, clearer articulation, and richer production of harmonics.

Suggested exercises:

Nasal sounds /m/ and /n/

These can be combined with tongue movements and chewing.

  • “Ummmm” (sliding from high to low and from low to high).
  • “Ummmm” combined with chewing, both voiced and unvoiced.
  • Continuous production of “m” (mouth closed), “n,” or “ny” (as in “canyon”), sustained, modulated, or sung on scales.
  • M…BRRRR…M…BRRRR…M…BRRRR / M…DRRRR…M…DRRRR (a nasal sound combined with a trill).
  • Humming “mmm…” in a yawn position with the mouth closed, to create internal space and increase resonance.
  • “Mini-mini-mini-mini…aaaaaaaa” and “mah-nah-nyah, meh-neh-nyeh…” on a chanted voice: fine tuning through small, repeated emissions to train short phonatory units and a stable onset.
  • A nasal sound produced together with tongue clicks.
  • The siren technique: with the tongue in the position for the nasal “ny,” keep the mouth cavity closed off and glide through a wide range of pitches.

Benefits: softens the voice, reduces laryngopharyngeal resonance, increases maximum phonation time, and improves projection.

Tongue and lip trills

Keep the tip of the tongue vibrating on “TRRR,” prolonging the sound and ending on a vowel:

TRRR… A  ·  TRRR… E  ·  TRRR… I  ·  TRRR… O  ·  TRRR… U

Repeat the same process with the lips vibrating on “BRRR,” prolonging the sound and ending on a vowel:

BRRR… A  ·  BRRR… E  ·  BRRR… I  ·  BRRR… O  ·  BRRR… U

Benefits: mobilizes the mucosa of the vocal folds, balances the coordination of breathing, phonation, and articulation, and reduces phonatory effort.

Very-high-pitch technique

  • Counting numbers.
  • Vowels.

Benefits: relaxes the thyroarytenoid muscles, engages the cricothyroid muscles, and balances resonance in the modal register.

Vocalizing on vowel sequences

ee · ay · eh · ah · aw · oh · oo

eeeeeeeee  ·  ayyyyy  ·  ohhhhh  ·  ooooo

Benefits: improves control of the glottal source, reduces constriction in the vocal tract, sharpens vowel definition, stabilizes vocal quality, heightens vocal awareness, and balances resonance.

Articulation exercises

For the lyrics of a song to be understood, words and speech sounds must be articulated precisely. Shaping those sounds requires agile, toned, and released muscles.

The lips, cheeks, and tongue all need to be trained so that each sound can be placed at its correct point of articulation. Nor should the teeth be forgotten: they need proper care, both in terms of hygiene, to avoid inflammation and discomfort, and in terms of a healthy bite that does not strain the temporomandibular joint. All of these factors contribute to comfortable articulation.

A cork can be used to intensify the exercise. Bite down on the cork just enough to hold it in place. With the cork in place, read the tongue twisters below: first speak only their vowel sounds, then say the full sentences.

These English tongue twisters were chosen as equivalents of the original Portuguese set, working the same consonant clusters (pr, br, tr, cr, dr, gr, fr, fl, pl, bl, tl, cl, gl).

  1. The prestigious professor promptly prepared a profoundly practical presentation.
  2. Pretty Priscilla prances proudly past the prickly prairie primroses.
  3. Crisp crusts crackle and crunch as crafty Cressida crumbles crackers.
  4. Brave Brian broke the brittle brown bread on the bright brick bridge.
  5. Brenda's brothers bravely brushed the brindled bull's bristly brows.
  6. Trevor trusted the trembling troubadours to trade true tales of treasure and trouble.
  7. Drowsy Drew dreamed the dragon drank the dreary drizzle dripping down the drain.
  8. Proud Pedro dropped three dripping stones from the prickly quarry and tripped on the driftwood.
  9. Crazy crows crowd the crooked crypt, cracking crimson crab claws.
  10. Green grapes grow in great green groves where grumpy gray groundhogs grin and grumble.
  11. Fresh fried fish, fish fresh fried, fried fish fresh, fish fried fresh.
  12. A flea and a fly in a flue were imprisoned, so what could they do? Said the fly, “Let us flee!” “Let us fly!” said the flea, so they flew through a flaw in the flue.
  13. Plucky planes plunge and plane above the plateau, where plain planters plant plump plums.
  14. Please place the plaque on the plump, pleated pillow, and plead for a placid plan.
  15. Bleak black blizzards blow blue blankets over blushing, blond Blake.
  16. An athlete crossed the Atlantic in search of the lost Atlantis she had glimpsed in an old atlas.
  17. Clever Clara clearly declared that the cloudy clock was clicking clumsily.
  18. Gleeful Gloria glimpsed glittering globes glowing in the glassy glen.

Benefits: reduces hypertonicity and increases volume, projection, vocal endurance, and articulatory precision.

Deep-breathing exercises

  • Alternate between mouth and nose breathing; lengthen the spine while coordinating inhalation and exhalation.
  • Move the neck muscles (side tilts, forward and backward tilts, and rotation).

Palate sweep

Run the tip of your tongue along the roof of your mouth (the hard palate) back toward the soft palate, then circle the tongue around the vestibule, sweeping the “walls of the mouth,” while keeping your lips together.

Benefits: lowers the larynx and relaxes the extrinsic laryngeal muscles.

Vocalises

These are vocal exercises sung with an instrument, which keeps the singer in tune, trains the ear, and works the vocal folds at the same time.

Benefits: extends vocal range, promotes the lengthening and shortening of the vocal folds, refines the voice, and heightens awareness of pitch.

Special attention to the larynx

The larynx is one of the most important and delicate structures in our vocal system. It is extremely important that you learn to sense how it feels. To do so, use your own hand to move the larynx gently from side to side and up and down.

Exercises for the larynx

  • Silent yawn: yawn without making a sound, keeping the tip of the tongue from pulling back as it naturally would.
  • Little gasp: draw in air very gently, with just a hint of sound, as if mildly startled.
  • Vocal flex: open and close the throat (moving the vocal folds) very gently, without breathing.
  • Steamboat: blow out with puffed cheeks, making a very soft sound.
  • Grimace: stick your tongue out of your mouth forcefully, then relax.
  • Fish lips: move the orbicularis muscles of the lips in a relaxed way, silently overarticulating the vowels “OO-AH-EE, OO-AH-EE” (like a drawn-out “why, why”).
  • Grimace with fish lips (chewing): combine the last two movements, using the tongue and the lip muscles at the same time, while counting softly to 10.
“All of these exercises should be performed with close attention to breathing and to the use of the diaphragm, controlling the exhaled air without forcing the throat.”

Vocal cool-down

A vocal cool-down readjusts the speech and articulation system from the singing voice back to the speaking voice, reducing phonatory effort and softening the voice.

  • Yawning technique: yawning reduces hard glottal attacks and medial glottal compression, widens the vocal tract, lowers the larynx, improves source-filter tuning, and raises the soft palate.
  • Head rolls on the vowels /a/, /o/, and /u/: dynamic relaxation and body-voice integration.
  • Nasal and/or trilled sounds on descending glides: soften the voice and reduce laryngopharyngeal resonance.
  • Chanted voice: reduces overall effort.
  • Fingertip massage of the face and neck: reduces hypertonicity and lowers the fundamental frequency.
  • Spontaneous speech, such as recounting an experience, to discriminate between phonatory settings (singing versus speaking): readjusts the speech organs for the speaking voice, stabilizes vocal quality, and balances resonance.

Chapter 3Posture, Support, and Body Awareness

One of the most common concerns in a singer's vocal work is the voice “going to the throat,” the place where vibration happens. More specifically, we are talking about the vocal folds, which vibrate to produce sound and should therefore be kept free of any tension.

Nevertheless, a point of support is needed to project the voice as far as one wishes (GAVA JÚNIOR; FERREIRA; ANDRADA E SILVA, 2010). As a rule, singers turn the throat into that point of support. This contradiction is why they end up with bulging throats and protruding veins. Our approach to support is the pressure that can be generated between the two diaphragms: the thoracic diaphragm and the pelvic diaphragm. The singer should apply slight abdominal pressure in the lower belly, roughly four finger-widths below the navel, followed by a slight contraction of the glutes (BJORKNER, 2008).

It is a bit like the subtle tummy tuck people do at the beach when someone pulls out a camera: a light drawing-in of the belly, without involving the stomach area, followed by a subtle squeeze of the glutes. This should become part of the singer's daily life, a natural, constant habit. It is to this pelvic region that singers should direct their attention whenever they want stronger, more sustained projection (COELHO, 1994).

The pelvic girdle support, or “pelvic belt,” as we like to call this bodily engagement, is also indispensable for stage speech. When an actor moves into shouting or whispering, maximum engagement of the pelvic belt is recommended. In this situation, negative pressure draws a large volume of air into the lungs. Actors very often raise their shoulders before shouting without pelvic support, placing that support in the throat instead; great care, observation, and practice are therefore recommended. When support is handled by the pelvic belt, the ribs take charge of drawing in and expelling the column of air (BJORKNER, 2008).

As air passes through the vocal folds, it sets them vibrating and produces the fundamental, or initial, sound, which is then amplified and shaped as it continues along its outward path. The paranasal sinuses and the sternum play a major role in amplifying the sound. Shaping is the work of the lips, the tongue, the soft palate, and the other structures of the mouth and nose.

Pelvic belt exercise

  • Draw the belly in (four finger-widths below the navel or, if you prefer, just above the pubic bone).
  • Engage the glutes, squeezing and releasing the buttocks.
  • Keep the knees soft and unlocked.
  • While walking, squeeze the buttocks alternately.

Body and interpretation

When we sing, we cannot always summon the emotion needed for the body to respond naturally; so we often fall back on forced, stereotyped gestures that convince no audience. Moreover, points of tension anywhere in the body are undesirable, because any physical imbalance can compromise vocal quality and performance.

“Hence the need to become aware of the body and to free it, so that there can be true ‘surrender’ in the interpretation […]. If I am aware of my movement, my body will know how to behave and convey the intended message… When you truly believe in what you are singing, the emotion and feeling are transmitted from the inside out, so that the body speaks, whether still or in motion […]”MARSOLA; BAÊ, 2000

Stretching and loosening the body

Body exercises

  • Body: standing with your legs together and your arms at your sides, begin to inhale through your nose. By the end of the inhalation, your hands should be joined and your arms stretched overhead, with your heels raised as high as possible. Hold your breath for 3 seconds, then release the air gently through your mouth, as if blowing, while lowering your arms and heels.
  • Neck: standing or seated, with your arms raised out to the sides at shoulder height and your hands on your chest, begin a slow inhalation through your nose while stretching your neck forward until your chin touches your chest. Hold the air for 3 seconds, then return to the starting position, blowing the air out gently and lengthening your neck.
  • Head rolls: gently turn your head to the right and to the left; then tilt it to each side, forward, and back, repeating each position four or five times and feeling the stretch in your neck muscles.
  • Face: inhale and, with your mouth closed, humming on “m,” move your facial muscles slowly and broadly, as if chewing.
  • Massage and activate the soles of your feet with the palms of your hands.
  • Rotate your ankles.
  • Loosen your knees: bend them slightly, place your palms on them, and circle them from side to side.
  • Make circles with your hips, then a sideways figure eight.
  • Bend your torso forward and let it hang, swaying gently from side to side.
  • Raise your shoulders one at a time, letting each drop gently. Repeat with both together.
  • Stretch your body up onto one foot while the other foot moves as if kneading the floor. Repeat on the other side.
  • Circle your tongue inside your mouth 10 times in each direction. Move it quickly from side to side and, finally, lower your head, letting your tongue hang out as far as you can and holding that position for a few seconds. Raise your head without straining, gently drawing your tongue back in. Repeat a few times.

Four movements to relax the neck

  • The “yes” movement: up and down.
  • The “no” movement: left to right.
  • The “maybe” movement: ear toward shoulder, alternating sides.
  • Circles: in both directions.

Chapter 4Vocal Hygiene and Voice Education

Voice education includes explaining how phonation works and what vocal health means. Simple, accurate explanations of how sounds are produced, together with the basic rules of vocal hygiene and correct voice use, help raise the patient's awareness, enabling them to avoid episodes of dysphonia and to curb vocal abuse.

Vocal hygiene programs rest on the premise that vocal abuse contributes to the development of voice problems, and that eliminating or reducing these harmful behaviors therefore supports vocal rehabilitation and prevention. Numerous reports in the literature indicate that vocal hygiene programs are effective in preventing and eliminating vocal abuse.

The explanation can conclude by showing how the different speech sounds are articulated in the mouth and why it matters that these movements be precise, so that speech is clear and intelligible and the message gets across.

How the voice is produced

Voice production begins in the brain, which sends impulses to the larynx, the organ that produces the voice. The larynx sits at the front of the neck, is shaped like a tube, and can be felt with the fingers. Inside this tube are the vocal folds, popularly known as the vocal cords. The term “vocal cords” is inaccurate, because they are not strings but two folds of tissue, lying parallel to the ground, that vibrate as air from the lungs passes between them.

You can feel these structures vibrate by placing your hand on your larynx while producing any sound, such as a prolonged “ah.” Producing voice, then, requires a command from the brain and air setting the vocal folds into vibration. Air is the fuel of voice production. Any imbalance in this mechanism, such as too much force or too much air, can lead to a voice problem. If too much air comes from the lungs, the voice will be breathy and the air will be audible in the sound; if, on the other hand, more muscular force is used than necessary, the sound will be squeezed into too little air and the voice will come out strained or hoarse.

The sound generated in the larynx is not the voice we hear in the room: it resembles the buzz of an electric shaver. This raw sound passes through a natural loudspeaker formed by the pharynx, mouth, and nose, where it is amplified. These structures also modify the raw sound, mainly through movements of the tongue and lips, and in this way the vowels and consonants that form words are produced.

Vocal health can be defined as a person's ability to vary their voice in quality, pitch, loudness, and inflection according to the setting, the situation, and the context of communication.

Hydration

Mainly since the 1990s, adequate hydration has been recognized as a vocal hygiene measure and an excellent adjunct to voice therapy. Hydration treatment includes a range of measures, such as humidifying the environment, inhaling cool or warm vapor directly through the mouth and nose, breathing in fine water droplets, and taking mucolytic drugs (guaifenesin) to thin secretions.

Measures to control dehydrating conditions have also been suggested, such as avoiding very dry environments, smoking, alcohol, caffeine and other diuretics, antihistamines, nasal sprays and decongestants, and mouth breathing. For hydration, we generally recommend sipping water throughout the day (rather than drinking whole glasses at once), especially during sustained speaking. Cyclists' water bottles work well for this, since their spouts are very convenient to use and carry.

Another very useful strategy is to breathe in, through the nose, fine droplets of water from a piece of gauze or a thick paper tissue moistened with filtered or distilled water. The inhaled moisture travels directly to the larynx, and the droplets provide instant lubrication, helping to reduce the viscosity of the mucus. Naturally, your airways must be clear, free of allergic, inflammatory, or infectious conditions, before you try hydration by inhaling water. Both drying conditions and overhydration should be avoided; overhydration can even worsen vocal conditions by depleting the body's mineral salts.

Research update · Hydration

A systematic review published in the Journal of Voice concluded that systemic hydration (drinking water regularly throughout the day) is the simplest and most cost-effective way to improve voice quality, and that it should be part of every vocal hygiene program. Steam inhalation improved acoustic measures such as jitter, shimmer, and the noise-to-harmonics ratio, and nebulization with isotonic saline lowered phonation threshold pressure. Mouth breathing, by contrast, which dries the surface of the vocal folds, worsened these same measures as well as perceived phonatory effort (ALVES et al., 2019).

For public speakers

Teachers, lecturers, salespeople, customer-service staff, and communicators in general rely on their voices as working tools just as much as singers do. The principles in this chapter apply to you: sip water throughout the day, pause and vary your pace when you speak, avoid competing with background noise, breathe through your nose whenever possible, and warm up your voice before speaking for long periods. Clear communication depends less on speaking loudly and more on articulating well, projecting with support, and varying your intonation.

Chapter 5What Protects and What Threatens the Voice

Many everyday factors affect the voice: smoking, alcohol, pollution, drugs, allergies, poor vocal habits, air-conditioning, poor diet, lack of rest, ill-suited clothing, high-strain sports, hormonal changes, and medications. This chapter examines each of them in turn.

A note to the reader

The information in this chapter, especially regarding medications, is for educational purposes only and does not replace an evaluation by a physician or a speech-language pathologist. Do not start, stop, or change any medication without professional guidance.

Smoking

Smoke damages the entire respiratory system, the vocal folds above all, and can cause irritation, swelling, coughing, increased secretions, and infections. It also dries out the vocal folds, making it harder for them to vibrate. Smoking is considered one of the triggering factors for laryngeal and lung cancer, precisely because of the severe irritation it causes in these organs. Nonsmokers exposed to cigarette smoke can also develop problems, so not smoking indoors is a matter of respect for other people's health.

Alcohol

Drinking alcohol, especially distilled spirits, irritates the vocal apparatus much as cigarettes do, but its main effect is immunosuppression, that is, a weakening of the body's defense responses. Because the first drinks loosen the brain's cortical control, the drinker feels more uninhibited. Alcohol also mildly numbs the pharynx, and with sensation in this area reduced, a series of vocal abuses can go unnoticed. The consequences become evident only after the alcohol wears off.

Distilled spirits such as whiskey, vodka, cachaça (Brazilian sugarcane spirit), and brandy are worse for vocal health, irritating the tissues more intensely, especially when mixed with fermented drinks such as beer, champagne, and wine. There is a strong association between heavy consumption of distilled spirits and laryngeal cancer. The damage caused by alcohol and tobacco varies with the amount consumed, but other factors also influence the extent and severity of disease, including individual, constitutional, genetic, and family characteristics.

Finally, the habit some singers have of gargling with or drinking alcohol to “warm up the voice” is unwise. The surface effect of alcohol in the mouth and pharynx is a temporary numbness that genuinely dulls unpleasant sensations. As a result, the effort required to speak or sing goes unnoticed, and once the alcohol wears off, the consequences may include irritation of the vocal apparatus or a decline in voice quality.

Pollution and noisy environments

Pollution can cause acute or chronic changes in the voice and larynx. The most extreme cases involve fires, smoke, and chemical leaks, which can injure the entire respiratory tract. The use of dry ice and pyrotechnics at concerts and performances also irritates the vocal tract and can even injure the larynx; performers who work with theatrical smoke report more complaints and respiratory symptoms.

The link between pollution and voice disorders is not easy to prove, because it is complex and not necessarily direct: some pollutants reach the body directly when inhaled and deposited in the airways, while others act indirectly, through the bloodstream. Recent studies point to a higher incidence of airway cancers among people who work in places with airborne toxins and pollutants, such as insecticides.

Vocal and laryngeal symptoms include hoarseness, a feeling of irritation in the throat, coughing, difficulty breathing, and irritation of the tissues of the mouth, tongue, nose, and respiratory tract.

Exposure to noisy environments (noise pollution) can endanger not only hearing but also the voice. In a noisy setting, we reflexively raise our voices in an effort to communicate and be heard over the background noise: this is known as the Lombard effect.

Drugs

Marijuana is extremely harmful, irritating the mucosa not only through the smoke itself but also through toxins released by the burning paper in which the herb is rolled. In addition, pinching the joint between the fingers and the teeth while smoking greatly raises the temperature of the smoke, damaging the tissues of the vocal tract. Besides a lowering of vocal pitch, users may show imprecise articulation and changes in speech rhythm and fluency.

Snorting powdered cocaine can directly injure the mucosa of the vocal tract through irritation and marked vasoconstriction. Perforations of the nasal septum and ulcerations of the vocal fold mucosa are common. Cocaine use can also alter perception and sensory control, thereby reducing control of the voice and leading to vocal abuse.

Injected cocaine causes muscular hypotonia, producing vocal fatigue and making it difficult to sustain adequate, efficient communication, particularly in professional voice use.

Allergies

People whose allergies affect the respiratory tract are more prone to voice problems, in direct proportion to the severity of the allergy. The respiratory mucosa tends to swell, which hinders the free vibration of the vocal folds, and constant secretions can irritate the larynx directly.

Nasal obstruction and persistent swelling caused by allergies can impair nasal resonance, making it harder to project the voice into the space. This difficulty can in turn lead to compensatory vocal strain, especially in children and voice professionals. People with allergies, particularly voice professionals, should therefore follow their doctors' instructions carefully to prevent flare-ups.

The effects of allergies are especially limiting for singers, who may struggle with high notes, experience breaks in the voice, and lose vocal agility.

An additional problem is the use of medications such as decongestants and antihistamines, whose side effects, including drying of the mucous membranes, are harmful to the voice. It is therefore essential to keep allergies under control and, if possible, to identify the trigger. Environmental control is a powerful tool with no adverse side effects.

During an allergy flare-up, the speech-language pathologist's approach is to advise the patient to limit voice use to what is necessary while increasing hydration, so as to thin secretions and improve the conditions for mucosal vibration. When dryness is severe and the voice must be used professionally, topical hydration is recommended in addition to drinking fluids: steam inhalers used through the mouth and nose, or actively breathing in droplets of filtered water through the nose from soaked gauze held in the palm of the hand near the nostrils before heavy voice use.

Medications

The main medications to avoid because of their adverse effects on the voice:

  • Pain relievers: avoid medications containing acetylsalicylic acid, as they can cause vocal fold hemorrhage. For example, many patients take one or two Aspirin tablets a day without medical advice, having heard about the benefits of thinning the blood. Acetylsalicylic acid is known to promote tissue bleeding and can lead to submucosal hemorrhages in the vocal folds, contact ulcers, or small frank bleeds; it is also a known cause of stomach irritation and ulcers.
  • Nasal sprays: when overused, can cause dryness and swelling.
  • Cough suppressants: highly irritating; can dry out the vocal tract.
  • Decongestants, antihistamines, and corticosteroids: reduce respiratory tract secretions and cause dryness, as well as insomnia, irritability, stomach irritation, and tremors. The habit of receiving monthly injections, or an injection at every minor vocal change, can cause irreversible damage to the larynx, including atrophy of the vocal fold mucosa.
  • Antidiarrheals: also reduce secretions and dry out the mucous membranes.
  • Diuretics: reduce secretions, producing thick mucus and persistent throat clearing.
  • Vitamin C: in high doses, can dry out the vocal tract.
  • Tranquilizers, sedatives, and sleep aids: can interfere with the neural control of voice and speech production, affecting control of phonation and speech rhythm.

Air-conditioning

Tolerance of air-conditioning varies from person to person, but in general it is hard on the mucosa of the vocal folds: cooling the air also lowers its humidity, which dries out the vocal tract and leads to voice production with more tension and effort. If air-conditioning cannot be avoided, as in office buildings or in cars in large cities, the units should be properly adjusted and their filters cleaned regularly, and you should drink more room-temperature water.

Central heating and space heaters have the same drying effect. In these cases, small containers of water placed around the room can help raise the relative humidity, alongside good vocal hydration.

A healthy diet

Heavy, highly spiced foods slow digestion and restrict the free movement of the diaphragm, a muscle essential to breathing. Much of the body's energy is diverted to digestion, and vocal function suffers as a result. In addition, heavily spiced food promotes gastroesophageal reflux, especially with evening meals, which can irritate the mucosa of the vocal folds and lead to lesions in the posterior larynx.

Choose light foods, and fruits and vegetables chewed thoroughly: besides supplying good nutrients, they relax the muscles of the jaw, tongue, and pharynx, improving diction and leaving the body feeling light.

Avoid chocolate, fried foods, and highly spiced dishes before intensive voice use, as they promote reflux and can increase throat clearing and the feeling of phlegm in the throat. Nor should you lie down after meals; wait 2½ to 3 hours before doing so.

Research update · Milk and dairy

For a long time, singers were told to avoid milk and dairy products before singing, on the assumption that they increase mucus production. The available studies, however, do not confirm this effect in healthy adults. In a classic study, volunteers challenged with rhinovirus did not produce more nasal secretions or experience more congestion when they consumed more milk and dairy; those who believed that “milk makes mucus,” however, reported more coughing and congestion, without actually producing more secretions (PINNOCK et al., 1990; WÜTHRICH et al., 2005). In practice: if you notice that your saliva becomes thicker after drinking milk, it is worth avoiding it before singing or speaking in public; if you do not, there is no reason for a blanket restriction.

Hard candies, lozenges, and throat sprays may ease unpleasant sensations, but they end up masking the pain of vocal strain, further harming the condition of the mucous membranes.

Apples, with their astringent properties, are recommended for cleansing the mouth and pharynx. Citrus juices, particularly orange and lemon, help absorb excess secretions, but they should not be consumed in excess, to avoid triggering gastroesophageal reflux.

Ice-cold food and drinks can be harmful, because the thermal shock triggers an immediate discharge of mucus and swelling of the vocal folds; some people, however, are more sensitive than others, and this should be taken into account. So hold the first sips of anything ice-cold in your mouth for a few seconds before swallowing, to avoid an abrupt change in temperature at the vocal folds.

Clothing

Clothing can interfere with voice production in three negative ways: through compression, through allergies, and by encouraging poor posture.

Compression around the neck and abdomen causes vocal tension, so choose light, loose-fitting clothes that allow the body to move freely. Avoid accessories around the neck and waist.

Some people are allergic to certain fibers, such as wool. Laundry soaps and fabric softeners can also trigger allergic skin reactions that extend to the respiratory mucosa.

Poor body posture also encourages faulty voice production. For this reason, shoes should ideally be low-heeled and made of natural materials, such as leather. High heels force a tense posture to keep the body upright and consequently stiffen the whole body, particularly the diaphragm, making phonation more difficult.

Sports

Some sports are more beneficial to voice production than others. Swimming and walking are highly recommended: they stimulate circulation and the body as a whole and improve breathing. The activities to avoid are those that demand violent movements and concentrate muscular tension in the neck, back, shoulders, and chest, which ends up increasing tension around the larynx and encouraging a compressed, strained voice. These include boxing, tennis, volleyball, and weight training.

Hormonal changes

During menopause, the decline in female hormones may lower the pitch of a woman's voice. Older men, on the other hand, tend to show a rise in fundamental frequency. People who are physically fit, or whose voices are trained, tend to keep their voices youthful and healthy.

Voice disorders may also recur in the premenstrual period, during the first days of menstruation, during pregnancy, and with the use of oral contraceptives. Birth-control pills usually contain a high dose of progesterone and, although they generally do not have a masculinizing effect on the voice, some women report temporary vocal changes while taking them.

Women who experience premenstrual tension are more likely to show vocal signs linked to the menstrual cycle, such as slight hoarseness, a deeper voice, vocal fatigue, or loss of vocal power, as a result of swelling and hormonal changes.

In late pregnancy, or in the first days after childbirth, breathing is often poorly coordinated. The voice may be lower, hoarse, and tired, with shorter phonation times. Women who use their voices professionally may experience significant vocal limitations during this period.

Lack of rest

Setting the vocal folds into vibration to produce speech requires a great deal of energy, and vocal fatigue can follow excessive or very loud voice use. The fatigue can be so intense that it extends to the whole body.

Usually, after a good night's sleep, the symptoms of vocal fatigue disappear and the voice returns to normal the next day. A poor night's sleep can leave the voice hoarse, weak, and breathy in the morning. Adequate rest is recommended, especially when heavy voice use is planned for the following day.

Beyond physical rest, we must also consider vocal rest. After intensive voice use, the ideal is a period of rest or limited use lasting as long as the voice was in use. Relative vocal rest is recommended, meaning that you limit how much you speak and avoid speaking loudly.
