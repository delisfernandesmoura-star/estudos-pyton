Part III

Vocal Ornaments

Fry, twang, yodel, vibrato, melismas, cry and appoggiatura: the devices that give the singing voice its identity and expressive power.

Ornaments are what turn a technically healthy voice into a voice with a signature of its own. Before exploring them, it is worth recalling what the previous chapters have shown: nearly all of them depend on breathing, support and relaxation. Practice each technique slowly, without discomfort and, whenever possible, under the guidance of a voice teacher or a speech-language pathologist.

Chapter 12Fry

Fry, or creak (which speech-language pathologists call vocal crepitation and some linguists call creaky voice), is a low sound made of widely spaced pulses, reminiscent of a creaking door. It differs from sounds produced by air turbulence, such as the sibilant fricatives /s, z, ʃ, ʒ/, whose main source is the airstream striking an obstacle, the teeth (LADEFOGED; MADDIESON, 1996). In fry, the noise comes from the irregular vibratory pattern of the vocal folds themselves. Hollien and Michel (1968) described it as a true phonatory register rather than a pathological change.

Relaxed fry is characterized by a lower larynx position; predominant action of the thyroarytenoid (TA) muscle, especially its internal bundle; less involvement of the vestibular (false) folds; looser vocal fold edges within a more relaxed laryngeal framework; single glottal pulses; and low intensity.

Tense fry is characterized by a raised larynx, with a consequent increase in glottal adduction; action of the lateral cricoarytenoid (LCA) and external TA muscles; greater involvement of the vestibular folds; stiff vocal fold edges within a tenser laryngeal framework; double or even triple glottal pulses; and an intensity equal to, or slightly greater than, that of relaxed fry.

In American English, this crackling voice is called vocal fry; in British English, creaky voice. Both terms tend to be conflated with the pulse (basal) register. The authors stress, however, that the two are not necessarily equivalent: the pulse register is defined by a very low fundamental frequency (f0) with a predominantly creaky quality, but creak can appear at any frequency in the vocal range, not only in the pulse register. Thus, “the term vocal fry refers exclusively to creak in the pulse register, whereas the term creaky voice denotes creak introduced into any type of phonation.” Pulse-register sound is a vocal quality that often appears in relaxed voices, signaling low tension, or in tenser voices, conveying expressions of “surprise, admiration or suffering.”

Tense fry

In clinical practice, tense fry may be recommended in cases of glottal insufficiency caused by unilateral laryngeal paralysis, sulcus vocalis and abductor spasmodic dysphonia, with the aim of improving glottal closure and vocal stability.

Chapter 13Twang

Nasality is one of the most widely studied aspects of voice quality and has been described in phonetics since the seventeenth century. Attempts to explain it have produced a number of oversimplifications. The classic claim is that when the velum closes, speech is not nasal, and conversely, that when speech is not nasal, the velum is closed. Another oversimplification holds that nasal emission always increases nasality and, conversely, that nasality requires nasal airflow. Laver (1980) reminds us that nasality is, in essence, a matter of resonance.

According to Kayes (2004), twang is physiologically characterized by aryepiglottic constriction, a high larynx and a raised tongue dorsum. The thyroid cartilage may be tilted or neutral, which allows the vibrating mass of the vocal folds to vary. Aryepiglottic constriction tends to increase resistance at the point of vocal fold closure, so it is important not to push a great deal of air pressure when producing this quality. Twang can also be either nasal or oral.

How difficult it is to perceive and understand the mechanism of vocal resonance became clear in 1940, when Paget conducted an experiment with a resonator tube shaped to produce the vowel /a/. When the tube was squeezed right at its opening, it produced the nasal quality of the American twang, a sound associated with New Englanders and unmistakably characteristic of Western country singing. This confirmed his earlier observations that the perceptually nasal quality of twang comes not from nasal resonance but from a constriction in the pharynx (YANAGISAWA et al., 1989).

Twang is associated with “mask resonance” (“singing in the mask”): a bright, metallic sound (humming helps add that metallic edge). The base of the tongue may be raised gently, for a subtle boost in metallic quality, or raised high, for a strongly metallic sound. The tongue needs to be well conditioned, almost “worked out,” so that it can keep its base raised and spread; its upper sides should rest against the upper molars and never leave them while singing. The tip of the tongue should stay on the floor of the mouth.

Where the term comes from

Twang is one of the voice qualities systematized in the vocal training model developed by the American researcher and teacher Jo Estill, who studied the role of aryepiglottic constriction in producing a “bright” sound (YANAGISAWA et al., 1989; STEINHAUER; KLIMEK; ESTILL, 2017).

Chapter 14Yodel

The yodel (or yodelling) is a singing technique characterized by rapid, deliberate alternation between two registers, usually the modal register (chest voice) and the upper register (head voice). Register shifts are tied to changes in voice quality, and abrupt transitions between registers are usually perceived as more or less noticeable “breaks” in the voice. These breaks can happen involuntarily, but they can also be produced on purpose and even exploited artistically, as in yodelling or in certain styles of contemporary music.

The yodel and country music

Country was the music of everyday rural life among white Southerners in the United States, shaped by the music of the British Isles, by Appalachian music and by the hymns of the Puritan churches, kept alive by descendants of English settlers.

At first, country was known as folk songs and was later nicknamed “white blues,” because its lyrics dealt with rural life in a similar way. Over the course of the genre’s history it was also called hillbilly music and, finally, country music. White rural laborers were disparagingly called hillbillies (roughly, “backwoods folk”), which is why, as cities grew, “white blues” came to be known by that name.

One of the genre’s chief pioneers was Jimmie Rodgers, nicknamed “the Singing Brakeman” (MAZZOLENI, 2012). He became a benchmark for the genre thanks to the vocal inflection of his yodel, particularly in “Blue Yodel,” one of the first country songs to sell a million copies (MAZZOLENI, 2012). The kinship with the blues lies in the fact that these were songs with sad themes and the word “blue” in the title, performed with nothing but guitar accompaniment.

From Jimmie Rodgers onward, country acquired a distinctive trait that set it apart from Black blues: its way of singing. After him, other artists further distinguished themselves from the blues by singing in duos, as The Delmore Brothers did, for example.

Chapter 15Vibrato

Vibrato is a regular modulation of the fundamental frequency used by classical singers and by some popular singers. It is a kind of orderly physiological tremor that makes the fundamental frequency rise and fall over a small range several times per second. It is considered a form of tremor, but one whose sound waves are similar or identical in length and amplitude. It is important to remember that vibrato is not the same as a shaky voice, which stems from a lack of training.

The foundation of vibrato is breathing and vocal support. Without proper support, it will be hard to control vibrato and sustain notes; without good breathing, there will be no good vibrato. Ideal support, airflow and adequate resonance allow vibrato to emerge naturally.

Vibrato is used on phrase endings, on intermediate notes, in melismas, in improvisation or on specific vowels. Any agility technique, such as melismas and the appoggiatura, depends on complete control of the voice, which begins with control of vibrato.

Vibrato gives the voice expressive richness and great emotional power; it is a resource as important as it is complex. Its rate ranges from 2 to 8 Hz, with an average of 5 to 7 Hz observed in classical singers (SUNDBERG, 1987); its intensity varies by 2 to 3 decibels.

Vibrato is not a well-defined phenomenon and has been interpreted in various ways: as an oscillation of the cricothyroid muscles, as an alternating modulation of the cricothyroid and arytenoid muscles, as an alternating contraction of the laryngeal and diaphragmatic muscles, or as a tremor of the respiratory and vocal tract musculature. Vibrato generally diminishes at soft intensities and increases in loud sounds.

The oscillation seen in vibrato may synchronously involve other parts of the vocal tract, such as the jaw, the velum, the base of the tongue, the epiglottis and the lateral and posterior pharyngeal walls. In professionals with sound technique and great natural gifts, vibrato is quite regular and controlled, reflecting a natural coupling between the vocal tract and the larynx. An aperiodic vibrato, by contrast, usually results from insufficient breath support or forced use of the voice, and may also be related to nervous tension.

A vibrato slower than 5 Hz is considered slow and is used in jazz, blues and African American popular music. In Brazilian sertanejo (country) music, one sometimes hears a forced, exaggerated vibrato on practically every sustained vowel.

Curcio, Behlau and Pontes (2000) studied vibrato in three singing styles and found that vibrato rate is similar in opera, rock and sertanejo singing, although stability is greater in opera. This study supports the notion that vibrato is a well-defined neurological phenomenon that can be observed in all individuals.

Narrowband spectrograms display the individual harmonics of a sustained sung vowel, in which the presence of vibrato can be observed.

Beware of overuse

Vibrato is the foundation for other techniques, such as melismas, but it should be used with care. Many people who discover this technique apply it to everything, all the time, which becomes extremely unpleasant for the listener.

Chapter 16Melismas

Melismas are popularly known as “runs” or “riffs.” The technique consists of changing the note (the perceived pitch) of a syllable while it is being sung, and it is closely tied to vocal agility.

Melismas have their roots in Gregorian chant, in which choristers drew syllables out at length. With European colonization, enslaved Africans in the Americas were influenced by Gregorian and classical singing, giving rise to faster, intuitively improvised melismas that, blended into African American spirituals (gospel), acquired a swing that propelled and modernized black music. The styles of black music that use melismas include gospel, R&B, soul, neo-soul and blues.

The music of ancient cultures used melismatic techniques to induce a hypnotic state in the listener, useful for mystical initiation rites and religious worship. This quality can still be found in Indian and Islamic music.

Melismas are, more often than not, highly intuitive or learned by imitation. But for those who want to start learning them and get a sense of how to stretch a syllable without going off pitch, using the right notes, the way in is to begin by singing syllables over some of the scales typical of black styles: the blues pentatonic scale and the major and minor pentatonic scales.

Today

In contemporary pop and R&B, melisma is the hallmark of performers such as Whitney Houston, Mariah Carey, Beyoncé and Ariana Grande, and it influences singers all over the world, including in Brazil. That is precisely why it pays to practice carefully: fast, extended melismas demand support, accurate intonation and agility, and they become a source of tension when performed without preparation.

Chapter 17Cry

As its name suggests, cry draws on the act of crying. The technique consists of knitting the eyebrows, as in a crying expression, in order to achieve:

  • greater vocal muscle firmness;
  • better quality in the high notes;
  • vocal projection;
  • a more metallic sound (brilliance in the voice) across vocal functions or modes, even in more neutral phonation.

Where the term comes from

In Jo Estill’s model, cry (also called sob) is one of the voice qualities described. It is associated with tilting of the thyroid cartilage through the action of the cricothyroid muscle, which helps thin and lengthen the vocal folds (STEINHAUER; KLIMEK; ESTILL, 2017). The “crying” expression serves as a helpful image for reaching this adjustment.

Chapter 18Appoggiatura

The appoggiatura is a musical term for a note used to ornament the main melody. This secondary note may lie below or above the main note, but it must be a neighboring note, usually a second away. It is typically performed with emphasis and then resolves to the main note. There are short appoggiaturas, which resolve quickly, and long ones, which take up a significant part of the main note’s value.

At first glance, appoggiatura and melisma may seem to be the same thing, but they are not. A melisma may contain several appoggiaturas, but an appoggiatura on its own is not a melisma.

In popular singing, the appoggiatura opens up an enormous range of possibilities for the singer, and melisma itself would not exist without it.

How to practice the appoggiatura

  1. First of all, keep your vibrato in good shape: it is the “mother technique” of virtually every ornamentation technique in singing.
  2. Sing a section of the original melody.
  3. Once the melody is in your head, start looking for spaces where you can add a note; do this one note at a time and memorize everything.
  4. Repeat the process in other parts of the song.

This trains your mind to create and memorize interpretations that you build yourself.
